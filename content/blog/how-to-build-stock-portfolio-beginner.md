Your first portfolio does not need to be clever. It needs to be sound. Get the structure right and you can improve the details for years. Get it wrong and one bad stock can set you back a long way.

Here is a step-by-step way to build it.

## Step 1: Get your base in order

Before you buy a single share, answer three questions.

**What is this money for?** Retirement in 30 years is different from a house in three. Money you need soon should not ride on the stock market.

**How long can you leave it alone?** The SEC's Investor.gov calls this your time horizon. A longer horizon lets you ride out more ups and downs.

**How much loss can you stomach?** That is your risk tolerance. Be honest. Picture your account down 30% in a bad year. Would you sell in a panic? If yes, take less risk.

Also keep some cash outside your portfolio for emergencies. If a surprise bill forces you to sell during a market drop, you lock in losses you did not need to take.

## Step 2: Choose your mix first

Asset allocation means splitting your money among stocks, bonds and cash. Investor.gov describes it as a personal decision. It depends on your time horizon and risk tolerance.

This choice matters more than which stocks you pick. Stocks have historically offered higher growth but bigger swings. Bonds and cash are usually steadier but grow less. The blend sets how bumpy your ride will be.

Pick your mix before you pick your stocks.

## Step 3: Decide how you will hold stocks

You have two main options.

**Funds.** An index fund or ETF holds many stocks in one purchase. Investor.gov notes that funds make it easy to own a small slice of many investments. For most beginners, a low-cost broad market fund is the simplest way to get real diversification.

**Individual stocks.** Picking companies yourself takes more research and more names to diversify properly. More on that next.

Many people combine the two. A broad fund forms the core. A smaller slice goes to individual stocks they want to learn about.

## Step 4: Know how many stocks diversification really takes

You may have heard that 10 to 15 stocks is enough. The research says otherwise.

Meir Statman studied this in 1987. He found a well-diversified portfolio of randomly chosen stocks needs at least 30 stocks for a borrowing investor and 40 for a lending investor.

Later work raised the bar. Campbell, Lettau, Malkiel and Xu published a study in 2001. They found that individual stocks had become more volatile over time. As a result, the number of stocks needed to reach a given level of diversification had increased.

What this means for you: 10 or 15 stocks still leaves a lot of company-specific risk. One bad earnings report can hurt. If you want to own only a handful of names, hold them alongside a broad fund rather than instead of one.

## Step 5: Size your positions

The simplest start is equal weighting. If you put $5,000 into 10 stocks, each gets $500. It is not perfect, but it stops the most common beginner mistake. That is putting a huge share of your money into the one stock you are most excited about.

Set a personal cap too. For example, decide that no single stock will ever be more than a set percentage of your total portfolio. Pick a number that would not wreck you if that company failed. Write it down before you buy.

## Step 6: Spread across sectors

Ten tech stocks are not a diversified portfolio. They tend to rise and fall together.

Investor.gov suggests spreading investments within each asset class, including across industry sectors such as consumer goods, health care and technology. If one sector struggles, others may hold up better.

Check your funds too. Investor.gov warns that even several funds may overlap. Look at their top holdings to make sure you are not buying the same companies twice.

## Step 7: Rebalance on a schedule

Over time, winners grow and losers shrink. Your mix drifts. A 60% stock target can creep toward 80% after a strong market.

Rebalancing brings you back to your target. Investor.gov describes two common approaches. You can rebalance at regular intervals, such as every six or 12 months. Or you can rebalance when an asset class drifts past a set percentage.

You can rebalance by selling what grew, or by sending new money to what lagged. Before you sell, consider fees and taxes.

## The beginner mistakes that cost the most

**Buying what you have heard of.** A famous brand is not a reason to own a stock. Know why you own each one and what would make you sell.

**Checking too often.** Daily price moves are mostly noise. Watching every tick pushes you to trade on emotion.

**Selling winners too soon and holding losers too long.** Researchers Hersh Shefrin and Meir Statman named this the disposition effect in 1985. It feels good to lock in a gain and painful to admit a loss. Decide your exit rules in advance so feelings do not make the call.

**Skipping the plan.** Write down your mix, your position cap and your rebalancing rule. When markets get loud, follow what you wrote.

## Put it together

1. Set your goal, horizon and risk tolerance.
2. Choose your stock, bond and cash mix.
3. Use a broad fund as your core.
4. Add individual stocks only with clear position caps.
5. Spread across sectors and check fund overlap.
6. Rebalance on a schedule.

That is a sound first portfolio. Everything after that is refinement.
