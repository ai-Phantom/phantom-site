Most traders believe they have an edge. Very few have measured it.

A trade journal is how you measure it. It is a plain record of what you traded, why, and what happened. A notebook works. A spreadsheet works better. What matters is that you write every trade down and review it.

## What "edge" actually means

An edge means your trades make money on average over many repetitions, after costs. Not on one trade. Not in one good week. Over a large sample.

Win rate alone does not tell you that. You need expectancy. Expectancy is what you expect to make or lose per trade on average:

**Expectancy = (win rate × average win) − (loss rate × average loss)**

Example. You win 45% of your trades. Your average win is $300. Your average loss is $150.

- 0.45 × $300 = $135
- 0.55 × $150 = $82.50
- Expectancy = $52.50 per trade

That is a positive edge with a win rate under 50%. Without a journal you might have felt like a losing trader. With the numbers in front of you, the picture is clear.

## What to record for every trade

Keep it short enough that you will actually do it. For each trade, write down:

- **Date and time** of entry and exit
- **Ticker and direction** (long or short)
- **Entry and exit prices**, exact, not "around $220"
- **Position size** in shares or contracts
- **Planned stop and target** before you entered
- **Setup tag**, such as breakout, pullback or earnings
- **Reason for the trade** in one sentence
- **Result** in dollars and in R (your profit or loss divided by your planned risk)
- **Fees and slippage**, so your numbers match your statement
- **One line of reflection**: did you follow your plan?

The "planned stop" field matters most. It lets you compare what you meant to risk with what you actually lost. Gaps between those two numbers are where accounts quietly bleed.

Good records also help at tax time. You will need dates, prices and cost basis for every sale anyway.

## Why your memory is not enough

Your brain is a poor record keeper. It remembers big wins and painful losses. It forgets the dull middle where most trades live.

There is also a well-studied bias at work. Terrance Odean studied 10,000 brokerage accounts in a 1998 paper. He found investors sold winners much more readily than losers. This is called the disposition effect. He also found the winners they sold went on to outperform the losers they kept, on average.

You will not see that pattern in yourself without data. A journal makes it visible. Look at how long you hold losers versus winners. If losers stay open much longer, you have found something to fix.

## How much data you need

Here is the hard part. Small samples lie.

Say your true win rate is 55%. How much will your measured win rate bounce around? A rough 95% range is:

- After 30 trades: about ±18 percentage points
- After 100 trades: about ±10 percentage points
- After 300 trades: about ±6 percentage points

After 30 trades, a 55% strategy can easily look like 40% or 70%. That is pure noise. So do not judge a setup on a handful of trades. Do not abandon one after a bad week either.

This also matters when you split your data. Suppose your breakout trades show a higher win rate than your pullback trades. Check how many trades are in each bucket first. If each has 15 trades, the difference may mean nothing.

## The monthly review

Set aside time once a month. Go through these questions in order:

1. **Totals.** What is your net P&L after fees? How many trades did you take?
2. **Expectancy.** What is your win rate, average win and average loss? What is your expectancy per trade?
3. **By setup.** Which setup tags made money? Which lost? How many trades does each have?
4. **Rule breaks.** How many trades broke your plan? What did those trades cost you?
5. **Holding time.** Do you hold losers longer than winners?
6. **One change.** Pick a single thing to adjust next month. Just one.

The rule-break question is often the most valuable. Many traders find that their planned trades are fine. Their losses come from impulse trades, oversized positions or moved stops.

## Watch your trade count

More trading is not more edge. Brad Barber and Terrance Odean studied 66,465 households at a discount broker from 1991 to 1996. In their 2000 paper, the households that traded most earned an annual return of 11.4%. The market returned 17.9% over the same period. The authors point to trading costs and overconfidence.

Your journal shows your own version of this. If your busiest days are your worst days, slow down.

## Make it a habit

A journal only works if you keep it. Make it easy:

- Log each trade the moment you close it, not at the end of the week.
- Use the same template every time.
- Keep the reflection to one line.
- Protect your monthly review time like an appointment.

After a few months you will have something most traders never get: evidence. You will know which setups pay, which do not, and which habits cost you money.
