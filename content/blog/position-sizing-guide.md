Ask a new trader about their last trade. You will hear the entry price and the target. Ask an experienced trader. You will usually hear the size first, and how much they stood to lose.

That order matters. Your entry decides whether a trade has a chance. Your size decides what happens to your account when you are wrong. And you will be wrong often.

## Why size matters more than you think

Win rate alone does not tell you whether you make money. What matters is how much you win when you are right versus how much you lose when you are wrong.

Say you win 60% of your trades. If your losers are twice the size of your winners, you still lose money. Ten trades: six wins of $100 is $600. Four losses of $200 is $800. You are down $200 with a "good" win rate.

Flip it. Win only 40% of the time. Let winners run to $300 and cut losers at $100. Ten trades: four wins is $1,200. Six losses is $600. You are up $600.

Sizing is how you control the loss side of that equation. It is the part of trading you fully control.

## Risk per trade, not dollars per trade

The core idea is simple. Decide how much of your account you are willing to lose on one trade. Then work backward to the number of shares.

A common rule of thumb is to risk 1% to 2% of your account per trade. It is not a law. It is a starting point that keeps any single mistake small.

Here is the math:

- Account size: $50,000
- Risk per trade (1%): $500
- Entry: $100. Stop: $95. Risk per share: $5
- Shares: $500 ÷ $5 = 100 shares

Notice something. Those 100 shares cost $10,000. That is 20% of your account. Yet you are only risking 1%. Position size and risk are not the same thing. Your stop distance connects them.

If your stop were $2 away instead, you could buy 250 shares and still risk $500. A wider stop means fewer shares. A tighter stop means more.

## What a losing streak does to you

Losing streaks happen to every strategy. The question is whether you survive them.

Here is what ten losses in a row do to your account at different risk levels. Each loss is a percentage of what is left, so the damage compounds:

- 1% per trade: down about 9.6%
- 2% per trade: down about 18.3%
- 5% per trade: down about 40.1%

Now look at the climb back. A 10% drawdown needs an 11.1% gain to recover. A 40% drawdown needs a 66.7% gain. The deeper the hole, the harder the climb. Small risk per trade keeps you out of deep holes.

## Adjusting for volatility

Not every stock moves the same amount. A calm utility might move 1% on a normal day. A small biotech might move 6%. If you use the same stop distance on both, the volatile stock will hit your stop on ordinary noise.

One fix is to set your stop based on volatility. Many traders use the Average True Range (ATR). ATR is the average of each period's true range over a set number of periods, often 14. True range is the largest of three values: today's high minus low, today's high minus yesterday's close, or yesterday's close minus today's low.

The formula is:

**Shares = Risk amount ÷ (ATR × multiplier)**

Example with $500 of risk and a 2× ATR stop:

- Calm stock, ATR $2.50. Stop distance $5. Shares: 100
- Volatile stock, ATR $6.00. Stop distance $12. Shares: 41

Both trades risk the same $500. The volatile stock simply gets a smaller position. This happens automatically once you size this way.

## Your stop is a plan, not a guarantee

A stop-loss order is not a promise of your exit price. The SEC's investor bulletin on stop orders is direct about this. Once your stop price is hit, a stop order becomes a market order. The fill can be well below your stop, especially when a stock gaps on news or earnings.

So treat your planned risk as a floor, not a ceiling. If a stock often gaps, size it smaller. Be careful holding full size through earnings. A stop-limit order controls the price, but it may not fill at all. Neither order type removes gap risk.

## A word on the Kelly criterion

You may run into the Kelly criterion. It comes from a 1956 paper by John Kelly at Bell Labs. It gives the bet size that maximizes long-run growth when you know your exact odds.

A simple form is: f = p − (1 − p) ÷ b. Here p is your win rate and b is your average win divided by your average loss.

If you win 50% of the time and your average win is 1.5 times your average loss, Kelly says to risk about 16.7% per trade. That is far more than most traders can stomach.

The catch is that Kelly assumes you know your true edge. You do not. You have an estimate from a limited sample. Overestimate your edge and Kelly will oversize you. That is why traders who use it often bet a small fraction of the Kelly number, if they use it at all.

## Watch position drift

Sizing is not only an entry decision. Positions change size as prices move. A stock you bought at 5% of your account can rally to 12% without you buying another share.

That is a good problem. But it is still more risk sitting in one name. Review your position weights on a schedule. Monthly works for most people. Decide in advance at what weight you will trim.

## A simple checklist

Before every trade, answer these:

1. How much of my account am I willing to lose on this trade?
2. Where is my stop, and why is it there?
3. How many shares does that give me?
4. Could this stock gap through my stop? If yes, should I size smaller?
5. After this trade, how concentrated is my account?

If you cannot answer all five, you are not ready to place the order.
