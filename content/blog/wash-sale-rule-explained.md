Selling a losing position can lower your tax bill. A realized capital loss offsets capital gains. But there is a rule that stops you from selling for the loss and simply buying the same thing right back. It is called the wash sale rule.

The rule lives in Section 1091 of the Internal Revenue Code. The IRS explains it in Publication 550. This post walks through what it says, where active traders trip over it, and what it does not settle.

This is general education about U.S. federal tax rules. Your situation may differ. A tax professional can tell you how the rule applies to you.

## What the rule says

According to IRS Publication 550, a wash sale happens when you sell or trade stock or securities at a loss, and within 30 days before or after the sale you:

1. Buy substantially identical stock or securities,
2. Acquire substantially identical stock or securities in a fully taxable trade,
3. Acquire a contract or option to buy substantially identical stock or securities, or
4. Acquire substantially identical stock for your IRA or Roth IRA.

The window runs 30 days before the sale and 30 days after it. Count the sale date too, and that is a 61-day window.

The "before" part surprises people. If you buy more shares and then sell your older, higher-cost shares at a loss within 30 days, that can be a wash sale too.

## It reaches beyond your own account

Publication 550 also says that if you sell at a loss and your spouse or a corporation you control buys substantially identical stock in the window, you have a wash sale.

So the rule looks across your household, not just one brokerage login. Your broker can only see the account it holds. It cannot see your spouse's account, your other broker, or your IRA. Keeping track is your job.

## What happens to the loss

In most cases the loss is not gone. It is postponed.

Publication 550 says you add the disallowed loss to the cost of the new shares. That becomes your new basis. You also add the holding period of the old shares to the new ones.

Here is the IRS's own example. You buy 100 shares for $1,000. You sell them for $750, a $250 loss. Within 30 days you buy 100 shares of the same stock for $800. You cannot deduct the $250 now. Your basis in the new shares becomes $1,050. When you finally sell those shares outside a wash sale, the deferred loss shows up.

So for most wash sales, the cost is timing. You lose the deduction this year and get it later.

## The IRA exception: the loss can be lost for good

There is one big exception. Publication 550 says the basis adjustment does not apply when the repurchase happens in your IRA or Roth IRA.

The IRS addressed this in Revenue Ruling 2008-5. If you sell at a loss in a taxable account and your IRA or Roth IRA buys substantially identical stock within the window, the loss is disallowed. And your basis in the IRA is not increased.

In plain terms, that loss is not deferred. It is gone. If you hold the same names in a taxable account and a retirement account, watch automatic purchases in the IRA closely.

## What counts as "substantially identical"

This is the gray area. The IRS does not give a bright-line list.

Publication 550 says you must consider all the facts and circumstances. It also says that, ordinarily, stocks of one corporation are not substantially identical to stocks of another corporation. So selling one company and buying a different company in the same industry is usually fine.

It also says bonds or preferred stock are not ordinarily substantially identical to the same company's common stock. The exception is convertible preferred or bonds that trade closely with the common. Those can be substantially identical.

What about funds? Say you sell one S&P 500 index fund and buy a different S&P 500 fund from another sponsor. The IRS has not issued a clear rule on funds that track the same index. Some practitioners treat that as risky. Swapping into a fund that tracks a different index is generally viewed as safer. If the answer matters to your tax bill, ask a tax professional before you trade.

## Traps that catch active traders

**Automatic dividend reinvestment.** If a dividend reinvests into the same stock within 30 days of a loss sale, that reinvestment is a purchase. It can make part of your loss a wash sale. Usually it is a small number of shares, but it still has to be reported correctly.

**Options.** Buying a call on the same stock within the window counts as acquiring an option to buy. Publication 550 also says the wash sale rules apply to losses on contracts and options to buy or sell stock.

**Partial repurchases.** If you buy back fewer shares than you sold, only the matching number of shares is affected. Publication 550 walks through matching examples. The rest of the loss can still be deducted.

**Frequent in-and-out trading.** If you trade the same ticker many times a month, wash sales can chain together. Each disallowed loss rolls into the next lot's basis. Your year-end loss can look very different from your trading P&L.

**What is not covered.** Publication 550 says the wash sale rules do not apply to losses on commodity futures contracts and foreign currencies. Dealers in securities are also excluded for business transactions.

## How much loss you can use

Even when a loss is allowed, there is a yearly limit on using it against ordinary income. IRS Topic 409 says that if your capital losses exceed your capital gains, you can deduct the lesser of $3,000 ($1,500 if married filing separately) or your net loss. Any excess carries forward to later years.

## A simple way to stay clear

- Before you sell at a loss, check what you bought in the last 30 days, in every account you and your spouse control.
- Turn off automatic dividend reinvestment on positions you may sell for a loss.
- After the sale, wait at least 31 days before buying back the same security, or buy something that is clearly not substantially identical.
- Keep your own trade records. Do not rely only on one broker's 1099-B.
