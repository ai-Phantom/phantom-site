## Two ways cash gets back to you

A profitable company can do four things with its earnings: reinvest in the business, pay down debt, pay a dividend, or buy back its own shares. The last two are the direct routes by which cash reaches shareholders, and they behave very differently in your account and on your tax return. A dividend shows up as money. A buyback shows up as a slightly larger slice of the company. Both are part of your return, and only counting the price change misses a large part of what you actually earned.

## Dividend mechanics

A dividend is a cash payment per share, declared by the board. Four dates govern it:

1. **Declaration date.** The board announces the amount, the record date and the payment date.
2. **Ex-dividend date.** The first trading day on which a buyer of the stock does not receive the dividend. To be paid, you must own the shares at the close of the day before.
3. **Record date.** The date on which the company's transfer agent checks the shareholder list. Since T+1 settlement began on 28 May 2024, the ex-date and the record date are the same day; under T+2 the ex-date was one business day earlier.
4. **Payment date.** When the cash arrives, typically two to four weeks after the record date.

On the ex-date the stock's reference price is adjusted downward by the dividend amount. If a stock closes at 100 dollars and goes ex by 1 dollar, the exchange's adjusted prior close is 99 dollars. The dividend is not free money: it is a piece of the company's cash moving from the corporate balance sheet to yours, and the share price reflects the transfer. Buying a stock the day before it goes ex to "capture" the dividend captures nothing except a taxable event.

## Yield, payout and coverage

**Dividend yield** = annual dividend per share ÷ share price. Use the forward annualised rate (the most recent quarterly dividend multiplied by four) unless the company pays irregularly.

**Payout ratio** = dividends per share ÷ earnings per share. It measures how much of each year's earnings is being paid out. A payout ratio above 100 percent means the dividend is being funded from cash reserves or debt, which is sustainable only briefly.

**Free cash flow coverage** = free cash flow ÷ total dividends paid. Earnings can be managed; cash is harder to fake. A dividend covered less than 1.2 times by free cash flow deserves scrutiny.

**The yield trap.** Yield rises when the price falls. A stock showing an 11 percent yield is usually not a bargain; it is a stock whose price has collapsed because the market expects the dividend to be cut. Check the payout ratio and coverage before the yield.

**Qualified dividends.** For US taxpayers, dividends from most domestic companies are taxed at the lower long-term capital gains rates if you hold the shares for more than 60 days within the 121-day window around the ex-date. Dividends from REITs and some foreign companies are not qualified. Lesson 11 covers the tax detail.

## Buyback mechanics

A share repurchase, or buyback, is the company using cash to buy its own stock in the open market and retire it. The share count falls. Each remaining share represents a larger fraction of the company, so earnings per share, dividends per share and your ownership percentage all rise even if the total figures are flat.

Rules that shape it:

- **SEC Rule 10b-18** provides a safe harbour from manipulation charges if the company follows limits on timing, price and volume (no more than 25 percent of average daily volume on most days).
- **Authorisations are not commitments.** A board authorising 100 billion dollars of buybacks may spend it over years or never complete it. Track executed repurchases in the cash flow statement, not the announcement.
- **A 1 percent excise tax** on net buybacks by US public companies has applied since 2023 under the Inflation Reduction Act.

When buybacks help you: the company buys shares below their intrinsic value, and the reduction in share count is real (not offset by stock issued to employees). When they hurt you: the company buys at high prices, funds them with debt, or simply mops up dilution from stock compensation. Check whether the diluted share count is actually falling year over year; that is the only proof a buyback is working.

## Total return

Total return = (ending price − starting price + dividends received) ÷ starting price, and conventionally assumes dividends are reinvested in more shares at the price on the payment date.

This matters more than beginners expect. A stock that pays a 3 percent yield and appreciates 5 percent a year has an 8 percent total return, and the reinvested dividends compound. Over decades, the reinvested-dividend component of index returns has historically been a large fraction of the total. Comparing a dividend stock's price chart to a growth stock's price chart, without adjusting for dividends, is comparing two different things.

Most quote screens show price-only charts. Most fund fact sheets show total return. Know which you are looking at.

## Chart

Picture a line chart with three lines over 20 years, starting at 10,000 dollars invested in a stock with a 3 percent dividend yield and 5 percent annual price growth.

- **Price only** (dividends ignored): 10,000 × 1.05^20 = 26,533 dollars.
- **Dividends taken as cash, not reinvested:** the price line above plus accumulated dividends. Each year's dividend is 3 percent of the growing share value, totalling about 9,920 dollars over the period. Combined: about 36,450 dollars.
- **Dividends reinvested** (total return): 10,000 × 1.08^20 = 46,610 dollars.

The gap between the first and third lines, more than 20,000 dollars on a 10,000 dollar start, is what a price-only chart hides. The gap between the second and third, about 10,000 dollars, is compounding: dividends buying shares that pay dividends.

## Worked example

Apple (AAPL) in fiscal 2024, from the Form 10-K for the year ended 28 September 2024, priced at the 31 December 2024 close of 250.42 dollars.

**Dividends.** Apple paid 0.25 dollars per share quarterly during calendar 2024, having raised it from 0.24 in May 2024. Annualised: 1.00 dollar. Total dividends paid in the fiscal year: 15.2 billion dollars.

- Yield at 250.42: 1.00 ÷ 250.42 = 0.40 percent.
- Payout ratio on reported diluted EPS of 6.08: 1.00 ÷ 6.08 = 16 percent.
- Coverage by operating cash flow of 118.3 billion: almost eight times. The dividend is trivially safe; it is also trivially small.

**Buybacks.** Apple repurchased 94.9 billion dollars of its own shares in fiscal 2024, six times what it paid in dividends. The share count fell from 15.55 billion at the end of fiscal 2023 to 15.12 billion on the fiscal 2024 cover page, a reduction of about 2.8 percent. Net income fell 3.4 percent (from 97.0 to 93.7 billion) but diluted EPS fell only 0.8 percent (from 6.13 to 6.08), because the buyback absorbed most of the decline. The buyback "yield" (repurchases ÷ market cap) was about 2.5 percent, so the total shareholder yield was roughly 2.9 percent, seven times what the dividend alone suggests.

**Compare Coca-Cola (KO).** Coca-Cola paid 0.485 dollars per share quarterly in 2024, or 1.94 dollars annualised, and closed 2024 at 62.26 dollars: a yield of 3.1 percent. Its payout ratio on 2024 diluted EPS of about 2.46 dollars was roughly 79 percent, and 2024 was its 62nd consecutive year of dividend increases. Coca-Cola returns cash mostly by cheque; Apple returns it mostly by shrinking the share count. Neither is better in principle. The dividend investor gets cash flow and a tax bill every quarter; the buyback investor gets a rising stake and defers tax until sale.

**The total-return check.** Anyone comparing AAPL and KO on a price chart for 2024 would understate Coca-Cola's result by about three percentage points and Apple's by less than half a point. Over a decade, the same error compounds into a meaningfully wrong picture.
