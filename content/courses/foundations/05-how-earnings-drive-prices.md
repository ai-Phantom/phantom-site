## The one equation

A stock's price can always be written as two numbers multiplied together:

Price = Earnings per share × Price-to-earnings multiple

The first term is what the company earns for each share. The second is how many dollars the market will pay for each dollar of those earnings, which is a compressed statement about expected growth, risk, interest rates and mood. Every price move is a change in one term, the other, or both. Earnings season is when the first term gets updated and the market re-decides the second.

This matters for a beginner because it removes the mystery from a stock falling on "good" earnings. The earnings term went up; the multiple term went down further. The rest of this lesson explains why that happens and how to read the reaction.

## What "earnings" means

Earnings is net income: revenue minus every cost, including taxes and interest, over a period. Divided by the weighted average number of diluted shares, it becomes earnings per share (EPS). Public companies report it four times a year, within roughly 40 days of quarter end for the 10-Q and 60 to 90 days for the 10-K, and almost all of them release a press release with the headline numbers a few weeks before the formal filing.

Two versions appear in every release:

- **GAAP EPS**, calculated under Generally Accepted Accounting Principles, audited annually, comparable across companies.
- **Non-GAAP or adjusted EPS**, where management removes items it considers non-recurring or non-cash: restructuring charges, stock-based compensation, amortisation of acquired intangibles, legal settlements. The SEC's Regulation G requires a reconciliation to GAAP, and Lesson 6 covers how to read it. Consensus estimates are usually on the adjusted basis.

## Expectations are the price

By the time a company reports, the market has already priced a forecast. That forecast has three layers:

1. **Consensus.** The average of published analyst estimates for revenue and EPS. It is freely visible on your broker's research page.
2. **Guidance.** The company's own forecast, given at the previous report. Most large companies guide on revenue and margins for the next quarter and the full year, often as a range.
3. **The whisper.** What sophisticated investors actually expect, which is often above consensus for companies with a habit of beating. A company that beats consensus by two percent every quarter is expected to beat by two percent; a beat of one percent is a disappointment.

The stock reacts to the gap between the report and the expectation, and, more importantly, to the change in expectations for the future. That is why guidance dominates the reaction. Last quarter is already history; the next four quarters are what the multiple is paying for.

## The reaction matrix

The four combinations of results and guidance produce predictable patterns, though never guaranteed ones.

- **Beat and raise.** Results above consensus and guidance raised. Usually a rise, sometimes a large one, unless the stock ran up sharply into the print.
- **Beat and lower.** Results above consensus but guidance cut. Frequently a fall, and often a sharp one, because it suggests the beat was pulled forward from future quarters.
- **Miss and raise.** Results below consensus, guidance raised. Often a muted or positive reaction, because the future outweighs the past.
- **Miss and lower.** The worst case. Large declines, sometimes 20 percent or more for growth companies, are common.

A fifth pattern deserves its own name: **in-line and flat**. A company that meets consensus and reaffirms guidance can still fall five percent if the whisper was higher or if the stock had rallied into the report. "Priced in" is not an excuse; it is a description of the expectation the market had built.

## Timing and mechanics

Companies release earnings either before the open (typically 6:00 to 8:30 a.m. Eastern) or after the close (4:00 to 4:30 p.m.). Almost none report during regular hours, to avoid disorderly trading. The press release is furnished to the SEC on Form 8-K under Item 2.02, usually within minutes of publication.

An analyst call follows, normally 30 to 60 minutes later, with prepared remarks and then questions. Under Regulation FD, adopted by the SEC in 2000, management may not disclose material information to analysts privately. The public call and release are therefore the only channel, which is why the tone of the question-and-answer session can move the stock a second time after the headline reaction.

Because the release lands outside regular hours, the first reaction happens in extended trading with thin volume and wide spreads. The move at 4:05 p.m. can reverse by 9:35 a.m. once the call has been digested. Beginners who trade the first print in after-hours are paying the widest spreads of the year for the least information.

## Why the multiple moves

Even if earnings arrive exactly as expected, the P/E term changes when the report changes beliefs about:

- **Growth rate.** A company growing 30 percent deserves a higher multiple than one growing 5 percent. A quarter that shows growth slowing from 30 to 20 percent can cut the multiple by a third with no change to the current year's EPS.
- **Durability.** Evidence that a competitive advantage is eroding (margin compression, customer losses) lowers the multiple.
- **Capital intensity.** A company announcing much larger capital spending may see its multiple fall even as revenue guidance rises, because free cash flow per dollar of earnings is expected to shrink.
- **Rates.** Not company-specific, but a rise in interest rates lowers the multiple for every company by making future earnings worth less today. Earnings season during a rate shock can look uniformly bad regardless of results.

## Chart

Picture a bar chart with two bars for a hypothetical stock before and after a report. Before: EPS estimate 5.00 dollars, multiple 30, price 150. After: the company reports and guides to EPS of 5.50 dollars, a 10 percent increase, but slower growth causes the market to pay a multiple of 24. New price: 5.50 multiplied by 24 equals 132. The earnings bar grew 10 percent; the multiple bar shrank 20 percent; the price fell 12 percent. Every earnings-season "why did it fall on good numbers" question is this chart with different values.

## Worked example

NVIDIA (NVDA) reported first-quarter fiscal 2026 results on 28 May 2025, after the close. The relevant figures, from the company's press release furnished on Form 8-K:

- Revenue: 44.1 billion dollars, up 69 percent from the prior year and 12 percent from the prior quarter.
- The company's own guidance for the quarter, given in February 2025, had been 43.0 billion dollars plus or minus 2 percent. Revenue came in about 2.6 percent above the midpoint.
- Data centre revenue: 39.1 billion dollars.
- A 4.5 billion dollar charge related to H20 product inventory and purchase obligations after the US government imposed licence requirements on exports to China in April 2025.
- GAAP diluted EPS: 0.76 dollars. Non-GAAP diluted EPS: 0.81 dollars, which the company said would have been 0.96 dollars excluding the H20 charge.
- Guidance for the second quarter: revenue of 45.0 billion dollars plus or minus 2 percent, which the company stated reflected a loss of roughly 8.0 billion dollars in H20 revenue from the export restrictions.

How the market read it. The revenue beat against guidance was modest. The headline EPS of 0.76 dollars was below the consensus that had been formed before the charge was known, so a naive screen showed a "miss". But the market had already priced the China restriction, so investors looked through the charge to the 0.96 figure, and the 45.0 billion dollar guidance met or slightly beat what most analysts expected once the 8 billion dollar H20 loss was removed. The stock rose roughly 3 percent in the following regular session.

Now run the equation. Consensus for the following four quarters barely changed; the multiple held. Modest revenue beat, in-line guidance, a one-time charge that everyone had anticipated: the report changed almost nothing about the future, so the price changed only a little. Compare that to a company where the same headline "miss" had come with guidance below expectations: the multiple would have compressed and the fall could have been ten times larger. Same word, "miss", opposite outcomes, because the future moved differently.
