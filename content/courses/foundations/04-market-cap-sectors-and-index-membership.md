## Price per share tells you nothing about size

A 900-dollar stock is not "expensive" and a 9-dollar stock is not "cheap". The share price is the value of the company divided by an arbitrary number of slices. A company can split its stock ten-for-one overnight, turning one 900-dollar share into ten 90-dollar shares, and nothing about the business changes. What tells you the size of a company is market capitalisation, and what tells you what kind of business it is and who is forced to own it is its sector classification and index membership. This lesson covers all three.

## Market capitalisation

Market cap equals share price multiplied by shares outstanding. It is the market's estimate of what all the equity is worth. It is not what you would pay to buy the whole company (that requires adding debt and subtracting cash, which Lesson 7 covers as enterprise value), but it is the universal shorthand for size.

Two versions of the share count matter:

- **Shares outstanding.** Every share that exists, listed on the cover of the company's 10-K and 10-Q. This is what most quote screens use.
- **Float.** Shares outstanding minus holdings of insiders, founders, governments and other holders who do not trade. Float-adjusted market cap is what the S&P 500 and most modern indices use for weighting, because it reflects the shares actually available to buy.

The size buckets are conventions, not rules, and different index providers draw the lines differently. The common thresholds are shown in the table below. What matters more than the exact cut-offs is what each bucket implies: who owns the stock, how liquid it is, how much analyst coverage it has, and how violently it can move.

## Table

Conventional market-cap buckets, with characteristics that follow from size.

| Bucket | Approximate range | Typical traits | Example at 31 Dec 2024 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mega-cap | Above 200 billion dollars | Dozens of analysts, index-fund ownership 25 percent or more, spreads of a cent | Apple, about 3.79 trillion; Coca-Cola, about 268 billion |
| Large-cap | 10 to 200 billion | Full coverage, S&P 500 eligible, institutional core holdings | The bulk of S&P 500 members |
| Mid-cap | 2 to 10 billion | Fewer analysts, S&P MidCap 400 candidates, wider spreads | Most S&P 400 members |
| Small-cap | 300 million to 2 billion | Russell 2000 territory, thin books, large moves on small volume | Most Russell 2000 members |
| Micro-cap | Below 300 million | Little coverage, often OTC, frequent target of promotions and fraud | Not recommended for beginners |

> **Callout:** The SEC has repeatedly warned that micro-cap stocks are where most pump-and-dump schemes live, precisely because thin float and no coverage make prices easy to manipulate. Until you can read a filing confidently, stay above 2 billion dollars.

## Sectors: the GICS map

Every listed company is assigned to a sector under the Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS), maintained by S&P Dow Jones Indices and MSCI. The hierarchy has four levels: 11 sectors, 25 industry groups, 74 industries, and 163 sub-industries as of the 2023 revision.

The 11 sectors are Energy, Materials, Industrials, Consumer Discretionary, Consumer Staples, Health Care, Financials, Information Technology, Communication Services, Utilities and Real Estate.

Why you should care:

- **Correlation.** Stocks in the same sector move together far more than stocks in different sectors. Owning five semiconductor companies is one bet, not five.
- **Sector ETFs and rotation.** Large amounts of money move between sectors as economic expectations shift. A stock can fall for weeks with no company-specific news simply because its sector is being sold.
- **Valuation context.** A P/E of 30 is high for a utility and ordinary for software. Ratios only mean something against sector peers.
- **Classification surprises.** Amazon is Consumer Discretionary, not Technology. Alphabet and Meta are Communication Services. Visa and Mastercard moved from Information Technology to Financials in March 2023. When a sector ETF rebalances after a reclassification, it must buy and sell accordingly.

## Indices and why membership moves prices

An index is a list of stocks and a rule for weighting them. The three families a US beginner meets:

**S&P 500.** About 500 large US companies, float-adjusted market-cap weighted, chosen by a committee under published criteria. As of the 2025 methodology, a new addition needs an unadjusted market cap of at least 22.7 billion dollars (the threshold is reviewed periodically), positive GAAP earnings in the most recent quarter and over the trailing four quarters combined, at least 50 percent of shares in public float, adequate liquidity, and a US domicile. The earnings requirement is why some very large companies (Tesla for years, Palantir until 2024) were outside the index despite their size.

**Nasdaq-100.** The 100 largest non-financial companies listed on Nasdaq, modified market-cap weighted. Heavily technology-weighted by construction. Rebalanced annually in December with special rebalances when concentration limits are breached.

**Russell 1000, 2000 and 3000.** Rule-based, no committee. The Russell 3000 is the largest 3,000 US stocks; the top 1,000 form the Russell 1000 and the next 2,000 the Russell 2000, the standard small-cap benchmark. Reconstitution has been annual in June, with FTSE Russell moving to semi-annual reconstitution starting in 2026. Because the rules are public, traders position ahead of the changes.

**Dow Jones Industrial Average.** Thirty stocks, price-weighted, so a 400-dollar stock has ten times the influence of a 40-dollar stock regardless of company size. Historically important, analytically odd.

Membership matters because passive money is enormous. When a company enters the S&P 500, every fund tracking the index must buy it on or before the effective date, regardless of price. The stock typically rises on the announcement and sees a huge volume spike at the close on the effective date. Deletions work in reverse. The effect is well known and partly front-run, but it is real, and it is a good example of price moving for reasons unrelated to the business.

## Worked example

On 6 September 2024, S&P Dow Jones Indices announced that Palantir Technologies, Dell Technologies and Erie Indemnity would join the S&P 500 effective before the open on Monday, 23 September 2024, replacing American Airlines, Etsy and Bio-Rad Laboratories. Palantir had only recently met the requirement for positive GAAP earnings over the trailing four quarters; it had long exceeded the market-cap threshold.

Consider what the announcement obliged index funds to do. Apple's weight in the index that quarter was roughly 7 percent, so a fund holding 100 million dollars of S&P 500 exposure held about 7 million dollars of Apple. A new entrant with a float-adjusted market cap of about 60 billion dollars against a total index float-adjusted cap of roughly 47 trillion dollars would receive a weight of about 0.13 percent, so that same fund needed to buy about 130,000 dollars of it. Multiply across the estimated trillions of dollars tracking the index and the required purchases run into the billions, concentrated in a single closing auction.

Now apply the market-cap arithmetic to Apple itself on 31 December 2024: 15,115,823,000 shares outstanding (from the fiscal 2024 10-K cover) multiplied by the 250.42-dollar close gives a market capitalisation of 3,785 billion dollars, about 3.79 trillion. Coca-Cola, by contrast, had roughly 4.31 billion shares and closed at 62.26 dollars, for about 268 billion dollars. Apple's share price was four times Coca-Cola's, but Apple was fourteen times as large. The share count did all the work.

## Putting it together

Before you research any stock, write down three facts: its market cap bucket, its GICS sector, and which major indices hold it. Those three facts tell you who the natural owners are, how liquid it is likely to be, what its peer group is for valuation, and whether you should expect forced buying or selling on rebalance dates. They take two minutes to find and they filter out most of the stocks that a beginner should not touch.
