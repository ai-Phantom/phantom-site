## Two questions before every sale

Tax is not an afterthought to a trade. For a US taxpayer in a taxable account, the same 6,000 dollar gain can cost 900 dollars or 2,200 dollars depending on two facts: how long you held the position, and what account it sat in. This lesson covers those two questions at a beginner level: what is taxed, when, at what rate, and how the choice of account changes the answer. It is general information about US federal rules, not advice for your situation; state taxes and personal circumstances change the numbers, and the thresholds below are for the 2025 tax year and are adjusted each year.

## Capital gains: short-term versus long-term

When you sell a security for more than its cost basis (what you paid, including commissions), the difference is a capital gain. Sell for less and it is a capital loss. Nothing is taxed until you sell; an unrealised gain, however large, creates no tax.

**Holding period.** Count from the day after you bought to the day you sold.

- **Short-term:** held one year or less. Taxed as ordinary income, at the same marginal rates as wages: 10 to 37 percent in 2025.
- **Long-term:** held more than one year. Taxed at preferential rates: 0, 15 or 20 percent depending on taxable income.

For 2025, the long-term rate is 0 percent up to taxable income of 48,350 dollars (single) or 96,700 dollars (married filing jointly), 15 percent up to 533,400 dollars (single) or 600,050 dollars (joint), and 20 percent above. High earners also owe the 3.8 percent net investment income tax on investment income above 200,000 dollars of modified adjusted gross income (single) or 250,000 dollars (joint).

The one-day difference between "one year" and "more than one year" is the single most expensive mistake a beginner can make with a large gain, and it is entirely avoidable.

## Netting and the 3,000 dollar rule

At year end, short-term gains and losses are netted against each other, then long-term gains and losses, then the two results against each other. If the net result is a loss, you may deduct up to 3,000 dollars of it against ordinary income (1,500 if married filing separately). Any remaining loss carries forward to future years without expiry.

This makes losses valuable. Selling a losing position to realise the loss, and using it to offset gains elsewhere, is called tax-loss harvesting. It is legal and routine, subject to one rule.

## The wash-sale rule

If you sell a security at a loss and buy the same or a "substantially identical" security within 30 days before or after the sale, the loss is disallowed for that year. Instead, it is added to the cost basis of the replacement shares, so you recover it when you eventually sell those. The window is 61 days in total: 30 before, the day of, and 30 after.

Two traps:

- **It applies across all your accounts**, including your spouse's and your IRA. A loss in a taxable account followed by a purchase in an IRA is disallowed and, because the IRA has no basis to adjust, permanently lost.
- **Dividend reinvestment plans** can trigger it. A reinvested dividend that buys shares within the window after a loss sale is a purchase.

Brokers report wash sales on your 1099-B for identical securities within the same account. They do not track across accounts or across similar-but-not-identical funds. That is your job.

## Dividends and interest

Dividends are taxed in the year received, whether or not reinvested.

- **Qualified dividends**, from most US corporations and many foreign ones, are taxed at the long-term capital gains rates, provided you held the stock for more than 60 days within the 121-day period starting 60 days before the ex-dividend date.
- **Non-qualified (ordinary) dividends**, including most REIT distributions and dividends on stock held too briefly, are taxed as ordinary income.

Your broker reports both on Form 1099-DIV. Interest on cash balances and bond funds is ordinary income, reported on 1099-INT.

## Cost basis and lot selection

Every purchase creates a tax lot with its own date and price. When you sell part of a position, which lot you sell determines the gain and the holding period. The default at most brokers is **first-in, first-out (FIFO)**: the oldest shares go first. You may instead elect **specific identification** before the trade settles, choosing the lot with the highest basis (smallest gain) or the one that has crossed the one-year line. Set the preference in your broker's account settings; it cannot be changed after the fact.

## Retirement accounts

An individual retirement account (IRA) is a wrapper: the same stocks and ETFs inside, but no annual tax on gains, dividends or interest. The tax is either paid upfront or at withdrawal.

**Traditional IRA.** Contributions may be deductible from current income (subject to limits if you or your spouse have a workplace plan). Growth is untaxed. Withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income, and withdrawals before age 59 and a half generally carry a 10 percent penalty on top. Required minimum distributions begin at age 73.

**Roth IRA.** Contributions are after-tax and not deductible. Growth is untaxed. Qualified withdrawals, after age 59 and a half and at least five years after the first contribution, are entirely tax-free. Contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn at any time without tax or penalty. Eligibility to contribute phases out at higher incomes: for 2025, between 150,000 and 165,000 dollars of modified adjusted gross income for single filers, and 236,000 to 246,000 for joint filers.

**Limits.** For 2025, total IRA contributions across traditional and Roth are capped at 7,000 dollars, or 8,000 if you are 50 or older. Employer 401(k) plans have a separate 23,500 dollar employee limit for 2025, with a 7,500 dollar catch-up at 50 and above. The IRS adjusts all of these annually; verify the current year's figures on irs.gov.

**Trading inside an IRA.** Buying and selling inside the account generates no taxable events and no 1099-B. There is no wash-sale tracking because there are no deductible losses. Margin borrowing is generally not permitted, and short selling is not available. The pattern day trader rule does not apply because there is no margin account, but settled-funds rules do.

> **Callout:** The general principle: hold assets you expect to trade often, and assets that throw off ordinary income, in tax-advantaged accounts; hold long-term positions and qualified-dividend payers in taxable accounts. Which assets and which accounts is a decision for you and, if the sums are large, a tax professional.

## Worked example

You bought 100 shares of Apple (AAPL) on 2 January 2024 at that day's closing price of 185.64 dollars, for a cost basis of 18,564 dollars. On 31 December 2024 AAPL closed at 250.42 dollars. Assume you are a single filer with 120,000 dollars of taxable income, which places you in the 24 percent ordinary bracket and the 15 percent long-term capital gains bracket for 2025, and that you are below the net investment income tax threshold.

**Gain.** 100 × (250.42 − 185.64) = 6,478 dollars.

**Sell on 31 December 2024.** Holding period: counting from 3 January 2024, the position has been held 364 days, not more than one year. Short-term. Tax: 6,478 × 24 percent = **1,554.72 dollars**.

**Sell on 3 January 2025.** Now the holding period is more than one year. Long-term. Tax at 15 percent: **971.70 dollars**. Waiting three calendar days (one trading day, since 1 January is a holiday) saves 583 dollars, at the risk of whatever the stock does on 2 and 3 January. On 2 January 2025 AAPL fell 2.6 percent, to 243.85; the gain would have shrunk to about 5,821 dollars, and the tax at 15 percent to about 873 dollars. Net of tax you would still have been ahead by about 25 dollars versus selling on 31 December, a coin flip. The lesson is not "always wait"; it is that the one-year line is worth about 9 percentage points of the gain and should be a conscious input to the exit decision.

**Same trade inside a Roth IRA.** Tax: zero, on either date. The 1,555 dollars (or 972) stays invested. Over 20 years at 7 percent, 1,555 dollars compounds to about 6,000 dollars. That is the value of the wrapper for one trade.

**A wash-sale variant.** Suppose instead AAPL had fallen and you sold on 31 December at a 2,000 dollar loss, then bought it back on 15 January 2025 because you still liked it. The loss is disallowed for 2024 and added to the basis of the January shares. If the repurchase was in your IRA instead, the 2,000 dollar loss is gone for good.
