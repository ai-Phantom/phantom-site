## The routine is the plan's operating system

Lessons 7 and 8 gave you rules and devices. A routine is the schedule on which they run: when the plan is read, when size is computed, when the screen is on, when it is off, when the journal is written and when the trader sleeps. It is boring by design, and the evidence in this lesson says the boring parts are load-bearing. The two variables that the studies in lesson 1 identified, frequency and cost, are both functions of how many hours a trader spends in front of a live tape and in what condition.

## Screens produce trades

Barber and Odean's 2002 paper, "Online Investors: Do the Slow Die First?", followed 1,607 investors at a discount broker who switched from phone-based to online trading in the early 1990s. Before switching, this group had been unusually good: they beat the market by more than 2% a year. After switching, they traded more actively, more speculatively and less profitably, and lagged the market by more than 3% a year. The authors' explanation combines overconfidence (the switchers had just had a run of success), the illusion of control from the interface (lesson 4's skill cues, now on a screen), and the simple fact that a faster, cheaper, always-available channel lowers the cost of acting on any impulse.

Their 2008 paper, "All That Glitters", measured what the impulses land on. Across three data sets of individual investors, buying was concentrated in attention-grabbing stocks: those in the news, on abnormally high volume, or with extreme one-day returns. Selling showed no such pattern, because you can only sell what you already own, while buying requires a search, and the search stops at whatever is loudest. Institutions did not show the pattern. The attention-driven purchases did not, on average, earn excess returns.

The trading implication is that time at a live screen outside the plan's setup window is exposure to a frequency generator. A plan whose setups fire only before 11:30 has no use for the screen after 11:30; the screen after 11:30 has plenty of use for the trader.

## Sleep produces the trader

Lesson 6 gave Venkatraman and colleagues' scanner result: after 24 hours without sleep, subjects anticipating gains showed heightened reward activity and their response to losses was blunted. Lim and Dinges' 2010 meta-analysis of 70 studies quantified the cognitive cost of short-term sleep deprivation across domains. The largest and most consistent effect was on sustained attention, the ability to keep watching for a signal over time; working memory and processing speed were also reliably impaired, while some higher reasoning tasks were less affected. Sustained attention is the faculty a rule-based session uses: the trader is a monitor, waiting for a defined condition and ignoring everything else. It is the faculty that goes first.

The routine treats sleep as an input to the session and records it. A trader who logs six hours or fewer does not trade that day, and the plan says so. This is not a wellness suggestion; it is the same logic as the daily loss limit, applied one night earlier.

## The three blocks

Pre-market, planner present, 30 to 45 minutes, ending before the open. Read the plan, aloud if it helps. Log last night's sleep; apply the sleep rule. Write down prior-day closing equity and compute risk dollars. Write the daily loss limit in R and dollars, and the maximum trades. Load the bracket template and check the exit legs are good-till-cancelled. Set an alarm for the setup window's end and one for any time stop. Note any scheduled event (an economic release, an earnings date) that the plan says to avoid. Do not read news beyond that; lesson 5 explained what stories do.

Session, doer present, the setup window only. The screen is on from the first minute the plan can fire to the last, and off outside that. Every order goes through the lesson 8 checklist, read aloud. After two consecutive losses, the plan's pause. At the loss limit or the trade cap, the platform closes; that is a physical act, not a mental note. If the plan has a time stop after the setup window, one alarm brings the trader back for that single action.

Post-market, planner returning, 20 to 30 minutes, within an hour of the last exit. Every trade logged with the fifteen fields, including the adherence flag and the cost, while the fills are still in front of you. A one-line note on the routine itself: did each block run as scheduled. Then the platform is closed for the day and the charts are not reopened. The weekly review from lesson 9 is a fourth block on a fixed day with the platform closed throughout.

## Worked example

Two versions of the same trader and plan, one week, with the numbers.

The plan fires only between 09:45 and 11:30 Eastern, at most two trades a day, and has a 15:55 time stop. Account $32,000, 1R = $320.

Trader A follows the routine: screen on 09:45 to 11:30, an alarm for 15:55. Screen hours per day: 1.75 plus a few minutes at 15:55; per week about 9. Trades per week: the plan generates a signal on roughly 60% of sessions, so about 3 trades. Each is a plan trade with the plan's measured expectancy, +0.155R from lesson 9's log: 3 x 0.155 x $320 = $149 expected per week.

Trader B keeps the screen on for the full session, 09:30 to 16:00, 6.5 hours a day, 32.5 per week. The plan trades are identical: 3 per week, +$149 expected. The extra 23.5 hours are exposure in the Barber and Odean sense. Suppose they produce two additional unplanned trades a week (a conservative number for a trader watching a tape for 23 hours), each on an attention-grabbing move with no defined edge and, being unplanned, no bracket. Give them a zero gross expectancy and lesson 8's un-bracketed loss profile on the losers: 50% win at +1R, 50% loss at -1.5R (a stop widened once). Expectancy per unplanned trade = 0.5 x 320 - 0.5 x 480 = 160 - 240 = -$80. Two per week: -$160.

Trader B's week: +$149 - $160 = -$11 expected. Trader A's: +$149. The plan is the same; the routine is the difference, and it is the entire difference between a positive and a negative expected week. Over 48 trading weeks, A expects +$7,152 and B expects -$528, on the same plan, same account, same measured edge.

Now sleep. Suppose on one day in five the trader has slept under six hours. On those days, the Venkatraman shift means losses feel smaller and the Lim and Dinges result means the setup is watched less reliably. If the effect is only that adherence on those days falls from 95% to 80%, and lesson 9's split holds (rule-followed rows +0.235R, deviations -0.90R), the expected result of a sleep-short day's trade is 0.8 x 0.235 + 0.2 x (-0.90) = 0.188 - 0.180 = +0.008R, against +0.235 x 0.95 + (-0.90) x 0.05 = 0.223 - 0.045 = +0.178R on a rested day. The sleep rule, "under six hours, no trades", forgoes +0.008R once a week to protect a routine that earns +0.178R the other four days. It costs nothing measurable.

Finally, Barber and Odean's own numbers on the switchers, translated. A 5-percentage-point swing (from +2% to -3% against the market) on a $32,000 account is $1,600 a year, produced by nothing but an always-available screen and the trades it generated.

## What the routine protects

Every earlier lesson's rule assumed something about when the trader is available and in what state. The disposition-effect rule assumes the exit was placed before the position existed: that is the pre-market bracket template. The sizing device assumes the equity input is yesterday's: that is the pre-market computation. The loss limit assumes a physical exit: that is the session block's end condition. The journal assumes the log is written from fills, not memory: that is the post-market block. The streak table assumes the trader reads it before the streak, not during: that is the weekly review. The routine is the set of appointments at which the planner shows up to do each of those things, and the set of hours during which the doer is not given a screen to do the opposite.

The measure of the routine is the same as the measure of the plan: did each block run, logged as a yes or no next to the day's trades. A week in which the blocks ran and the trades lost is a good week for the process. A week in which the blocks did not run and the trades won is the online switchers' first month.
