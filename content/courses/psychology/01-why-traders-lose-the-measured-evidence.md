## Start with the base rate, not the story

Every course on trading psychology opens with a story about fear and greed. This one opens with a table, because the studies that measured what retail traders actually do found something more specific than fear and greed: they found a process. The average retail trader trades too often, pays more in costs than any edge is worth, holds losers and sells winners, and keeps going without ever measuring whether the whole thing works. Each of those is a behaviour, and each has been counted.

You will see the same names repeatedly in this course. Brad Barber and Terrance Odean obtained the trading records of tens of thousands of accounts at a large US discount broker and published a series of papers through the 2000s. Fernando Chague and colleagues did the same with the complete record of everyone who began day trading Brazilian equity-index futures. European and US regulators now require brokers in some products to publish the share of their clients who lose. Those three sources are the evidence base for this lesson. Nothing here is a motivational claim; every number has a paper behind it.

## Barber and Odean: trading is hazardous to your wealth

The 2000 paper of that title studied 66,465 households with accounts at a large discount broker from 1991 to 1996, a period in which the US market returned 17.9% a year. The average household earned 18.7% gross and 16.4% net. The 2.3-point difference is the cost of trading.

The important result comes from sorting households by how much they traded. The least active fifth turned over about 2% of their portfolio per month and netted 18.5% a year, slightly ahead of the market. The most active fifth turned over more than 250% of their portfolio a year and netted 11.4%, 6.5 points behind the market. Gross returns across the five groups were not reliably different. In other words the active traders were not picking worse stocks; they were paying more to hold the same kind of stocks for shorter periods.

The paper estimates that a round trip cost about 3% in commissions and about 1% in bid-ask spread for that era's discount broker. Commissions have since fallen to zero at most US brokers, and this course does not pretend otherwise. But the mechanism is not the commission; it is that every trade has a cost, that costs scale with frequency, and that the frequency was not chosen by any rule. When the same authors looked at what happened to investors who switched from phone to online trading (a 2002 paper you will meet in lesson 11), turnover rose and returns fell again, with lower commissions. Cheaper trading produced more of it.

The 2001 follow-up, "Boys Will Be Boys", sorted the same accounts by gender. Men traded 45% more than women, and trading reduced men's net returns by 2.65 percentage points a year against 1.72 for women. The authors' explanation is overconfidence, which is lesson 4. For now, note the shape of the finding: the more a group traded, the more it lost, and the extra trading was not driven by better information.

## Chague, De-Losso and Giovannetti: day trading for a living

The Barber-Odean households were mostly investors who traded too much. The Brazilian study looked at people who set out to trade for a living. Using the regulator's complete record of every individual who began day trading the mini Ibovespa futures contract between 2013 and 2015, the authors followed 19,646 people from their first day.

Most quit quickly. Only 1,551, fewer than 8%, persisted for more than 300 trading days. Of those persistent traders, 97% lost money. Only 1.1% (17 people) earned more than the Brazilian minimum wage, about R$54 per day at the time. Only 0.5% (8 people) earned more than the starting salary of a bank teller, about R$310 per day. The authors also tested whether performance improved with experience. It did not: average daily losses grew, not shrank, as people persisted.

Read that carefully because it is the most important number in the course. The traders who kept going were not the ones who had found an edge; they were the ones who had not stopped. Persistence, on its own, was a losing behaviour. Something other than time in the market has to be doing the selecting, and the rest of this course is about what that something is: a written plan, measured expectancy, and a stop rule that fires on evidence rather than on hope.

## What the brokers must disclose

Two regulators now require the base rate to be printed on the product. In 2018 the European Securities and Markets Authority restricted the sale of contracts for difference to retail clients, and its decision (Decision (EU) 2018/796) records why: analyses by national regulators found that 74% to 89% of retail CFD accounts lost money, with average losses per client between 1,600 and 29,000 euros. Every EU and UK CFD broker now carries a sentence of the form "X% of retail investor accounts lose money when trading CFDs with this provider". The UK Financial Conduct Authority's own analysis, published in its 2016 consultation, found 82% of clients lost, with an average loss of £2,200.

In the US, Commodity Futures Trading Commission rule 5.5 requires retail forex dealers to disclose, for each of the past four quarters, the percentage of non-discretionary accounts that were profitable. You can read those figures on any registered forex dealer's disclosure page. They are quarterly, they vary, and they are consistently a minority.

These disclosures are about leveraged products with wide spreads, and losing "in a quarter" is not the same as losing over a career. But the direction of every disclosure matches the two academic studies: for the population of retail accounts, the typical outcome is a loss, and the size of the loss scales with activity.

## Worked example

Take the Barber-Odean average household and check whether costs alone explain the gross-to-net gap.

Annual turnover: 75% (the paper's average of about 6.5% per month, multiplied by 12 is 78%; the authors round to 75%). Round-trip commission cost: about 3%.

Cost drag = turnover x round-trip commission = 0.75 x 3% = 2.25 percentage points per year.

Observed gap: gross 18.7% minus net 16.4% = 2.3 percentage points. The arithmetic accounts for the gap without any assumption about skill.

Now the most active fifth. Turnover: more than 250%. Cost drag at 3% per round trip = 2.5 x 3% = 7.5 percentage points before the bid-ask spread. Observed shortfall against the market: 17.9% minus 11.4% = 6.5 points. Again, cost alone is enough; no stock-picking deficiency is required to explain the result.

Finally, translate to a modern account with zero commissions. Suppose a $25,000 account, 250% turnover, and an all-in cost of 0.10% per round trip (spread plus slippage on liquid US shares; wider for options or small caps). Dollar value traded per year = 2.5 x $25,000 = $62,500 in round trips. Cost = $62,500 x 0.10% = $62.50, or 0.25% of the account. That is small. But the Brazilian day traders were trading dozens of times a day, not 2.5 times their account a year. At 20 round trips a day on a $25,000 account with a 0.05% all-in cost each, daily cost = 20 x $25,000 x 0.05% = $250, which is 1% of the account per day and roughly 250% per year. No commission is needed for cost to dominate; frequency does it on its own.

## What this says about the average process

Look at what the studies did not find. They did not find that the losing traders were stupid, or poor, or uneducated; Barber and Odean's households were affluent, and the Brazilian traders were self-selected for motivation. They did not find that losses came from a few catastrophic mistakes; they came from the steady drag of frequency and cost, and from holding the wrong positions for the wrong length of time (lesson 2). And they did not find that experience fixed anything, because the traders did not measure, so there was nothing for experience to act on.

So the average process, reconstructed from the data, is: trade whenever something looks interesting, with a size that feels right; sell what is up and keep what is down; do not record costs; keep going as long as the account allows. Every clause of that sentence is a rule that could be written the other way round. Trade only a defined setup. Size from the stop. Exit by a rule set before the entry. Log every trade and its cost. Stop on a written trigger. That is the whole course, and the rest of it is the evidence for each clause and the arithmetic that makes it work.

Your task for this lesson is to measure your own turnover, because you cannot know which fifth of the Barber-Odean sample you resemble until you have counted.
