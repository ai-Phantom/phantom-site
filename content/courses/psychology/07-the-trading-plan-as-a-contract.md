## A plan is a contract between two people

Richard Thaler and Hersh Shefrin's 1981 paper, "An Economic Theory of Self-Control", modelled a person as two agents. The planner cares about the long run and is the one who decides, on Sunday, to trade only one setup with a 1% risk. The doer lives in the present and is the one who, on Wednesday at 10:47 with three red bars on the screen, wants to widen the stop. The planner cannot be present at 10:47. What the planner can do is write rules, and arrange for the rules to bind.

That is what a trading plan is. It is not a statement of intent, and it is not a description of a style. It is a contract written by the planner, in advance, in terms precise enough that the doer's only remaining job is execution. Every lesson so far has ended with a rule; this lesson is about what the full set of rules looks like, how precisely they need to be written, and why the writing has to happen before the session.

## The evidence that written rules bind

Peter Gollwitzer's work on implementation intentions is the most direct evidence. A goal intention is "I will exit losers promptly". An implementation intention is "if the price touches $48.50, then I sell 200 shares at market". The second form links a specific cue to a specific action, and Gollwitzer's 1999 review showed that this form is executed far more reliably than the goal form: the cue becomes salient, the action becomes automatic, and deliberation at the moment of action, which is where every bias in lessons 2 to 6 operates, is skipped.

Gollwitzer and Sheeran's 2006 meta-analysis pooled 94 studies with over 8,000 participants and found an average effect size of d = 0.65 for implementation intentions on goal attainment, a medium-to-large effect by the usual conventions, and one that held across health, academic and consumer domains. The effect was strongest exactly where goals were hard to reach because of competing impulses. A trading plan written as a set of if-then rules is a set of implementation intentions, and the meta-analysis is the evidence that the form, not just the content, matters.

Note what this does not claim. It does not claim a plan makes a setup profitable; only the journal (lesson 9) can say that. It claims that a plan written in the if-then form will be executed more consistently than one written as intentions, which is the precondition for the journal measuring anything at all.

## What a complete plan contains

A plan is complete when two traders reading it would place the same orders at the same times in the same sizes, and when it says what to do in every state the account can be in. Concretely, it has nine sections.

Markets and hours. Which instruments, which session, and nothing else. "SPY and QQQ, regular session 09:30 to 16:00 Eastern, no trades in the first five minutes."

Setups. Each defined by observable conditions, at most three. Lesson 5 explained why more conditions weaken the thesis rather than strengthening it. "Setup A: first 5-minute close above the 15-minute opening-range high, before 11:30."

Entry. The exact order: type, price, timing. "Buy at the next 5-minute bar's open, market order."

Stop. Placed with the entry, as an order. "Stop at the opening-range low, entered as a stop-market order in the same ticket."

Target and time stop. "Target at entry plus one times (entry minus stop), limit order. If neither is hit by 15:55, exit at market."

Size. A formula, computed before the open from a number that cannot change intraday. "Risk 1% of prior-day closing equity. Shares = risk dollars / (entry minus stop), rounded down."

Daily limits. Lesson 6's circuit breakers. "Session ends at -2R realised. Maximum four trades. After two consecutive losses, 20 minutes away."

Review cadence. When the journal is read and what can change. "Every trade logged within 30 minutes of exit. Weekly review Sunday: expectancy, standard error, adherence. At most one rule changed per month, and only if the log has 30 trades since the last change."

Stop rules. Lesson 12's drawdown rule. "If the account draws down 12R from its peak, live trading stops; return criteria as written in section 9."

## Worked example

Convert an incomplete plan into a complete one and compute the orders it generates.

The incomplete plan: "I trade breakouts in liquid stocks with tight stops and let winners run, risking a small amount per trade."

Every phrase requires a decision at the moment of action: which stocks, what is a breakout, how tight, how far is "run", what is small. The doer will make each decision under the conditions of lessons 2 to 6.

The complete version, for one setup:

Markets: SPY only. Hours: 09:45 to 15:55 Eastern.
Setup: first 5-minute close above the high of the 09:30 to 09:45 range, occurring before 11:30.
Entry: market order at the open of the next 5-minute bar.
Stop: stop-market at the 09:30 to 09:45 range low, submitted with the entry.
Target: limit at entry + 1.0 x (entry - stop). Time stop: market exit at 15:55.
Size: 1% of prior-day closing equity; shares = floor(risk dollars / (entry - stop)).
Daily limits: -2R realised ends the session; maximum two trades; no re-entry after a stop-out on the same setup.

Now apply it to a session. Prior-day closing equity $32,000, so risk = 0.01 x 32,000 = $320. The 09:30 to 09:45 range: high 64.00, low 62.40. The 10:00 bar closes at 64.10, above the range high, before 11:30: setup fires. Entry at the 10:05 open: 64.15.

Stop = 62.40. Per-share risk = 64.15 - 62.40 = $1.75. Shares = floor(320 / 1.75) = floor(182.9) = 182. Actual dollar risk = 182 x 1.75 = $318.50, which is 0.995% of equity.

Target = 64.15 + 1.0 x 1.75 = 65.90, limit order. Time stop 15:55.

Three orders go in one ticket: buy 182 at market, stop-market 182 at 62.40, limit sell 182 at 65.90. The doer's remaining tasks for this trade are: cancel the unfilled leg when the other fills (most platforms do this automatically with a bracket order), and exit at 15:55 if both are still open. There is no decision that takes the current P&L, the recent trade history or the news as an input.

Check the plan against each earlier lesson. Disposition effect: the stop and target are placed at entry, so no exit is decided in the presence of an open gain or loss. Loss aversion: 1R is $318.50, 1% of the account, which is small enough that the loss branch stays flat. Overconfidence: size is a formula, not a feeling, and the 1% cannot be raised intraday. Anchoring: 64.15 is not an input to any rule after the orders are placed. Revenge: -2R ends the session at -$637, and a second trade on the same setup after a stop-out is forbidden.

## Written before, not during

The plan is written before the session for the reason Thaler and Shefrin gave: the planner and the doer are different agents, and only the planner is available before the open. Every earlier lesson supplied a measurement of how the doer differs: a 2.25 loss-aversion coefficient, a five-bar sample with a 0.22 standard error, a 68% cortisol elevation with a 44% shift in risk appetite, a sleep-deprived brain that over-weights gains. A rule written under those conditions is written by the wrong party. It will say what the doer wants, in the doer's language, with the doer's exceptions.

The plan is also a fixed object, which is what makes the journal meaningful. If the rules change during the session, the journal records the performance of a moving target and can never accumulate the 100 trades that lesson 4 said any conclusion needs. One change a month, at 30 or more trades since the last, is the cadence in the table; it is slow on purpose. The setups you trade will feel obvious and the rules will feel restrictive; both feelings are the doer's, and neither is evidence. The next lesson turns the plan's rules into orders and checklists so that even the execution requires as little of the doer as possible.
