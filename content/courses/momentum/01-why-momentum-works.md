<p>Momentum — the tendency of stocks that have been rising to keep rising in the near term — is one of the most studied and most persistent anomalies in financial markets. It has been documented in every major market globally, across asset classes, and across time periods stretching back over 200 years. It should not exist in an efficient market. But it does.</p>
        <h2>The Academic Evidence</h2>
        <p>The landmark 1993 Jegadeesh and Titman paper documented that stocks with the best 3–12 month returns continued to outperform over the next 3–12 months by an average of 12% annually. This was not a fluke — it has been replicated in hundreds of studies across every major market globally and remains statistically significant through to the present day.</p>
        <h2>Why It Persists: Behavioural Finance</h2>
        <ul>
          <li><strong>Underreaction to news</strong> — Investors are slow to update their views. When a company reports strong earnings, the stock rises but the full implications for future earnings are not immediately priced in. The stock continues drifting upward for weeks as analysts revise estimates upward.</li>
          <li><strong>Institutional herding</strong> — Fund managers who see a stock performing well add to positions gradually. A stock appearing in more and more fund portfolios receives persistent buying pressure.</li>
          <li><strong>Trend-following algorithms</strong> — Quant funds explicitly buy winners and sell losers, creating a self-reinforcing feedback loop that extends trends beyond what fundamentals alone would justify.</li>
          <li><strong>Retail fear of missing out</strong> — As a stock rises, retail attention increases. Social media coverage increases. More buyers enter late in the move.</li>
        </ul>
        <h2>Why Most Retail Traders Fail to Capture It</h2>
        <p>Momentum requires buying stocks that are already up — counterintuitive for most people who want to "buy low." It requires holding through normal volatility without getting shaken out. And it requires a rules-based system, because the emotional temptation to take profits early or hold losers is overwhelming without written rules.</p>
