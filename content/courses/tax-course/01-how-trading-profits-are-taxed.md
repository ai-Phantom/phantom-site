## Before you start

This course is education, not advice. It covers United States federal income tax as it applies to an individual who trades in a personal account, using the Internal Revenue Code, IRS publications, form instructions and revenue procedures as its only sources. It does not cover state or city income tax, which in some states taxes capital gains at ordinary rates, and it cannot know your circumstances: filing status, other income, prior-year carryovers, entity choices and residency all change the answer. Every figure in the course carries its tax year and its source. Where a 2026 figure has been published it is used; where a lesson works an example, it uses tax year 2025 because that is the last complete year with real prices. Before you act on anything here, take it to a CPA or an enrolled agent who can see your whole return.

## Two questions decide the rate

When you close a position in a taxable account, the Internal Revenue Code asks two questions. Was the asset a capital asset in your hands? For an individual investor, and for a trader who has not made the section 475 election covered in lesson 7, shares, ETFs, options and bonds are capital assets, so the answer is yes and the profit is a capital gain. Second, how long did you hold it? IRC section 1222 divides capital gains and losses into short-term (held one year or less) and long-term (held more than one year). Short-term gains are taxed as ordinary income at the same graduated rates as wages. Long-term gains get the separate 0%, 15% and 20% schedule in section 1(h).

That single line, one year or less versus more than one year, is where most of the money in this lesson lives, so the counting rule matters. Publication 550 and the Form 8949 instructions both say: begin counting on the day after you acquired the property, and include the day you disposed of it. Buy on 2024-07-01 and the holding period starts on 2024-07-02. Sell on 2025-07-01 and you have held for exactly one year, which is not more than one year, so the gain is short-term. Sell on 2025-07-02 and it is long-term. The trade date, not the settlement date, is the date that counts for stock traded on an established market.

## The ordinary brackets

Short-term gains are added to your other ordinary income and run through the section 1 rate tables. For tax year 2025, Rev. Proc. 2024-40 (Table 3, single filers) sets the brackets at 10% up to $11,925 of taxable income, 12% to $48,475, 22% to $103,350, 24% to $197,300, 32% to $250,525, 35% to $626,350 and 37% above that. Married filing jointly (Table 1): 10% to $23,850, 12% to $96,950, 22% to $206,700, 24% to $394,600, 32% to $501,050, 35% to $751,600 and 37% above.

For tax year 2026, Rev. Proc. 2025-32 (section 4.01) moves the single brackets to $12,400, $50,400, $105,700, $201,775, $256,225 and $640,600, and the joint brackets to $24,800, $100,800, $211,400, $403,550, $512,450 and $768,700. These are taxable-income figures, after the standard deduction or itemised deductions. The standard deduction for 2025, as amended by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act and restated in Rev. Proc. 2025-32 section 3.01, is $15,750 single and $31,500 joint; for 2026 it is $16,100 and $32,200 (Rev. Proc. 2025-32 section 4.14).

The bracket rate is a marginal rate: it applies only to the dollars inside that band. A short-term gain that pushes taxable income from the 22% band into the 24% band is taxed partly at each.

## The long-term schedule

Long-term gains, together with qualified dividends, are taxed under section 1(h) at 0%, 15% or 20% depending on taxable income. Rev. Proc. 2024-40 section 2.03 sets the 2025 breakpoints: for single filers the 0% rate applies up to $48,350 of taxable income and the 15% rate up to $533,400, with 20% above; for joint filers the breakpoints are $96,700 and $600,050; for heads of household $64,750 and $566,700; for married filing separately $48,350 and $300,000. Rev. Proc. 2025-32 section 4.03 gives the 2026 breakpoints: single $49,450 and $545,500; joint $98,900 and $613,700; head of household $66,200 and $579,600; married filing separately $49,450 and $306,850.

Two mechanics are easy to get wrong. First, the rate applies to net capital gain, which is your net long-term gain reduced by any net short-term loss (section 1222(11)). Second, long-term gains are stacked on top of ordinary income. The Qualified Dividends and Capital Gain Tax Worksheet in the Form 1040 instructions taxes ordinary income first and then fills the 0%, 15% and 20% bands from wherever ordinary income left off. A single filer with $40,000 of wages after deductions and a $20,000 long-term gain pays 0% on the first $8,350 of the gain (up to the $48,350 breakpoint) and 15% on the remaining $11,650.

Topic No. 409 also lists the exceptions: collectibles gain and the taxable part of section 1202 small-business stock gain are capped at 28%, and unrecaptured section 1250 gain on depreciated real estate at 25%. None of these normally arise from trading listed securities.

## The net investment income tax

IRC section 1411 adds a 3.8% tax on the lesser of your net investment income or the amount by which modified adjusted gross income exceeds a threshold: $250,000 for joint filers and surviving spouses, $125,000 for married filing separately and $200,000 for everyone else. The thresholds are written into the statute and have never been indexed for inflation, so each year more filers cross them. Net investment income includes interest, dividends and net gain from the disposition of property not held in an active trade or business, which covers every trade in a personal account, short-term or long-term. It is computed on Form 8960 and, because it keys off MAGI rather than taxable income, a large gain can trigger it even in a year when your wages alone would not. Section 1411(c)(2) also brings in income from a trade or business of trading financial instruments or commodities, so a section 475 trader does not escape it.

## Worked example

Single filer, tax year 2025, $180,000 of wages, standard deduction $15,750, taxable income before trading of $164,250, which sits inside the 24% band ($103,350 to $197,300). Tax on that income from the Rev. Proc. 2024-40 Table 3 formula: $17,651 + 24% × ($164,250 − $103,350) = $17,651 + $14,616 = $32,267.

The position: 100 shares of SPY bought on 2024-07-01 at the Yahoo Finance daily close of $545.34, cost $54,534.00.

Scenario A, sold 2025-07-01 at the close of $617.65, proceeds $61,765.00. Gain = $61,765.00 − $54,534.00 = $7,231.00. Holding period: counting from 2024-07-02 through 2025-07-01 inclusive is exactly one year, so the gain is short-term. Taxable income becomes $171,481, still inside the 24% band. Tax = $17,651 + 24% × ($171,481 − $103,350) = $17,651 + $16,351.44 = $34,002.44. The gain cost $34,002.44 − $32,267.00 = $1,735.44, which is 24% of $7,231 exactly.

Scenario B, sold one trading day later, 2025-07-02, at the close of $620.45, proceeds $62,045.00. Gain = $7,511.00. The holding period now exceeds one year, so it is long-term. Taxable income of $171,761 is above the $48,350 zero-rate breakpoint and below the $533,400 top of the 15% band, so the whole gain is taxed at 15%: $7,511 × 0.15 = $1,126.65. The larger gain paid $608.79 less federal tax.

NIIT check: modified AGI in scenario A is $180,000 + $7,231 = $187,231, below the $200,000 single threshold, so no section 1411 tax. If wages had been $200,000, MAGI would be $207,231, the excess over the threshold would be $7,231, net investment income would also be $7,231, and the tax would be 3.8% × $7,231 = $274.78 on top of the income tax.

## What this means for how you trade

The tax code does not reward you for trading well; it charges you by holding period and by total income. A short-term gain is worth 76 cents on the dollar to a 24%-bracket trader and 59 cents to a top-bracket trader once the NIIT is counted; the same gain held past one year is worth 85 and 76 cents. That gap is the price of every exit you take before the anniversary, and the lessons that follow are about the rules that decide when a loss is allowed, when a gain is recognised, and which parts of that price you can legitimately avoid.
