## Investor, trader, dealer

IRS Topic No. 429 divides people who buy and sell securities into three classes. Investors expect income from dividends, interest or appreciation and hold for personal investment; they are not in a trade or business, and their sales are capital gains and losses subject to the section 1211(b) loss limit and the section 1091 wash-sale rule. Dealers under section 475(c)(1) have customers and are outside this course. Traders are in the business of buying and selling securities for their own account, which the law treats as a trade or business even though a trader has no inventory and no customers.

Topic 429 states the test as three conditions that must all be met: you must seek to profit from daily market movements in the prices of securities and not from dividends, interest or capital appreciation; your activity must be substantial; and you must carry it on with continuity and regularity. It then lists the facts and circumstances the IRS considers: typical holding periods, the frequency and dollar amount of trades during the year, the extent to which you pursue the activity to produce income for a livelihood, and the amount of time you devote to it. There is no published trade count, day count or hours figure. Topic 429 warns that calling yourself a day trader does not make you one, and that a taxpayer may be a trader in some securities and an investor in others, in which case the investment securities must be identified as such in your records on the day you acquire them, for example by holding them in a separate account.

Trader status is a factual conclusion you reach and defend, not an election you file. If your pattern is a few dozen swing trades a year around a day job, the IRS view in Topic 429 is that you are an investor.

## What trader status changes on its own

Less than most people expect. Without a further election, Topic 429 says a trader must still treat gains and losses as capital gains and losses on Form 8949 and Schedule D, and both the capital loss limitation and the wash-sale rules continue to apply. What changes is the expense side: a trader reports business expenses on Schedule C, which lesson 8 covers, while an investor's expenses are largely non-deductible. Commissions are never an expense for either; they adjust basis. Topic 429 also confirms that a trader's gains are not subject to self-employment tax, which has a consequence for retirement plans in lesson 10. The net investment income tax still applies, because section 1411(c)(2)(B) brings in a trade or business of trading financial instruments.

## The section 475(f) election

IRC section 475(f)(1) lets a person engaged in a trade or business as a trader in securities elect to recognise gain or loss on every security held in connection with that business at the close of the year as if it were sold for fair market value on the last business day of the year. Three things follow, all confirmed by Topic 429 and the Form 4797 instructions.

Gains and losses on trading-business securities become ordinary, reported on Form 4797 Part II line 10 with an attached statement, instead of capital gains on Schedule D. Losses are therefore not capped at $3,000; they offset wages and any other income in full, subject to the section 461(l) excess business loss limit, which for 2025 is $313,000 for a single filer and $626,000 on a joint return (Rev. Proc. 2024-40 section 2.32) and for 2026 is $256,000 and $512,000 (Rev. Proc. 2025-32 section 4.31); any excess becomes a net operating loss carryforward. The wash-sale rule does not apply to trading-business securities. Open positions are marked to market at year end, so the timing of the last trade of the year no longer matters.

The costs are equally clear. Every gain is ordinary: a trader with a section 475 election never gets the 0%, 15% or 20% long-term rate on trading-business securities, so a profitable trader whose winners run past a year gives something up. The mark to market pulls unrealised gains into income with no cash from a sale. And the election is sticky: section 475(f)(3) says it applies to the year made and all subsequent years unless revoked with IRS consent.

Securities held for investment are outside the election if identified on the day acquired (section 475(f)(1)(B)), which is how a trader keeps a long-term portfolio at capital-gain rates alongside a mark-to-market trading book. Section 475(f)(2) is a separate election for commodities, including section 1256 futures; a securities-only election leaves futures under the 60/40 regime of lesson 5.

## The deadline, and why it is the whole game

Topic 429 and Rev. Proc. 99-17 section 5.03 set the timing. For an existing taxpayer, the election statement must be filed by the due date, not including extensions, of the original return for the year immediately preceding the election year, attached either to that return or to a request for an extension of time to file it. To have the election effective for tax year 2026, the statement had to be with your 2025 return or extension request by April 15, 2026. To have it effective for 2027, file it by April 15, 2027 with the 2026 return or the Form 4868 extension. The statement must say that you are electing under section 475(f), the first tax year for which it is effective, and the trade or business it covers (Rev. Proc. 99-17 section 5.04).

A new taxpayer, one who was not required to file a return for the prior year, makes the election by placing the statement in its books and records within 2 months and 15 days after the first day of the election year and attaching a copy to that year's return. For an individual who traded last year and filed a return, this route is closed. Topic 429 says late elections are generally not allowed; miss the date and the earliest effective year is the one after next.

The election is also a change in method of accounting. Topic 429 requires Form 3115 under Rev. Proc. 2025-23 section 24.01 with the election-year return, with a section 481(a) adjustment for any unrealised gain or loss on the trading book at the start of the election year. Revocation needs a notification statement by the due date of the prior year's return under Rev. Proc. 2025-23 section 24.02 plus another Form 3115; revoke within five years of electing, or re-elect within five years of revoking, and the non-automatic procedures of Rev. Proc. 2015-13 with a user fee apply.

## Worked example

Single filer, tax year 2025, $150,000 of wages, standard deduction $15,750, and a trading year that lost $80,000 net across several hundred short-term trades, including $12,000 of losses on positions repurchased within 30 days.

As an investor (or a trader without the election): the $12,000 is disallowed under section 1091 and added to the basis of the replacement shares, leaving $68,000 of realised loss; section 1211(b) allows $3,000 against ordinary income. Taxable income = $150,000 − $3,000 − $15,750 = $131,250. Tax (Rev. Proc. 2024-40 Table 3) = $17,651 + 24% × ($131,250 − $103,350) = $17,651 + $6,696 = $24,347.00. Carryforward to 2026: $65,000 short-term (plus the $12,000 waiting in the replacement shares' basis).

With a section 475(f) election in effect for 2025 (filed by April 15, 2025 with the 2024 return): the wash-sale rule does not apply, so the full $80,000 is an ordinary loss on Form 4797 Part II. It is below the $313,000 section 461(l) limit, so all of it is allowed. Adjusted gross income = $150,000 − $80,000 = $70,000. Taxable income = $70,000 − $15,750 = $54,250. Tax = $5,578.50 + 22% × ($54,250 − $48,475) = $5,578.50 + $1,270.50 = $6,849.00. Nothing is carried forward.

Difference in 2025 federal tax: $24,347.00 − $6,849.00 = $17,498.00, plus the investor's $77,000 of deferred deductions, which will take years to use at $3,000 a year unless future gains absorb them.

The reverse case: had the same trader made $80,000 with 40% of it in positions held more than a year, the investor would pay 24% on $48,000 and 15% on $32,000 = $11,520 + $4,800 = $16,320, while the section 475 trader would pay 24% on all $80,000 = $19,200. The election is worth most to traders whose losses are large, whose gains are short-term anyway, and whose wash-sale exposure is heavy.
