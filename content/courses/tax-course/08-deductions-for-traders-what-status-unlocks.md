## Two very different starting points

For an investor, almost nothing you spend to trade is deductible. IRC section 212 allows expenses for the production of income in principle, but section 67 makes them miscellaneous itemised deductions, and section 67(g) disallows every miscellaneous itemised deduction for tax years beginning after 2017. The original provision was due to expire after 2025; Public Law 119-21 (the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, 2025) struck the sunset, so for 2025, 2026 and onward the disallowance is permanent. Market data, software, subscriptions, a home office, education, a tax preparer's fee for Schedule D: none of it reduces an investor's tax. Commissions and fees to buy or sell are not expenses for anyone; Topic 429 says they must be used to figure gain or loss, so they sit in basis and proceeds.

One deduction survives for investors. Section 163(d) allows investment interest, including margin interest on a taxable account, as an itemised deduction on Schedule A up to the amount of net investment income for the year, computed on Form 4952 and carried forward if limited (Publication 550, chapter 3). Net investment income for this purpose excludes long-term gains and qualified dividends unless you elect to include them and give up their lower rate. And because it is an itemised deduction, it only helps if your itemised deductions in total exceed the standard deduction, $15,750 single or $31,500 joint for 2025.

For a trader who meets the Topic 429 test in lesson 7, with or without a section 475 election, the expenses of the trading business are ordinary and necessary business expenses under section 162, reported on Schedule C. Topic 429 says so directly. The gains themselves still go on Schedule D or Form 4797, so a trader's Schedule C typically shows expenses and no revenue, a loss that offsets other income. That pattern is expected and is not itself a problem, but it is one reason trader status is examined closely.

## What a trader can deduct

The ordinary list: real-time market data and exchange fees; charting, scanning and back-testing software; a trade journal and tax-lot software; a dedicated computer and monitors (expensed under section 179 or depreciated); a share of internet and telephone; books and subscriptions; and the fee for preparing the business part of the return. Margin interest on the trading account is business interest rather than investment interest, deductible on Schedule C without the section 163(d) net-investment-income cap, although interest allocable to identified investment positions stays on Form 4952. Section 195 lets you deduct up to $5,000 of start-up costs in the year the business begins, reduced dollar for dollar above $50,000 of such costs, with the rest amortised over 180 months.

## The home office

Section 280A(c)(1) and Publication 587 allow a deduction for the part of your home used regularly and exclusively as your principal place of business. Exclusive means the space is not also the family room; regular means routine use, not occasional. For a trader who trades from home, the home is normally the principal place of business because that is where the work happens. Publication 587 offers two methods. The simplified method is $5 per square foot of qualifying space up to 300 square feet, a maximum of $1,500, with no depreciation and no Form 8829. The actual-expense method allocates rent or mortgage interest, utilities, insurance, repairs and depreciation by the business percentage of the home on Form 8829; it can be larger, but depreciation is recaptured when the home is sold. Under either method the deduction cannot exceed the gross income from the business use of the home, with a carryover of the excess under the actual method only; how a trader's gains reported on Schedule D or Form 4797 count toward that limit is a question to settle with your CPA before relying on a large home-office figure.

## Education

Treasury Regulation 1.162-5 draws a line that catches many traders. Education that maintains or improves skills required in your existing trade or business is deductible under paragraph (a). Education that is part of a program qualifying you for a new trade or business is personal under paragraph (b)(3) and is not deductible, however useful it was. A course, a mentorship or a seminar you paid for before you were in the trading business is on the wrong side of that line; the same spending after you are established and trading, to improve your existing skills, is on the right side. Section 195 can capture some pre-opening costs as start-up expenditures, but only costs that would have been deductible had the business already existed, which sends the same question back to Reg. 1.162-5.

## What trader status does not unlock

Trading gains are not self-employment income (Topic 429), so a trader does not pay self-employment tax and also cannot claim the deductions that depend on it: the deduction for one-half of self-employment tax under section 164(f), the self-employed health insurance deduction under section 162(l), and contributions to a SEP or solo 401(k), which need earned income (lesson 10). Section 199A's 20% qualified business income deduction does not help either: section 199A(d)(2)(B) names trading in securities as a specified service business, and capital gains are excluded from qualified business income in any case. Meals remain 50% deductible under section 274 and only when there is a business purpose with another person. Losses on the trades themselves are governed by lessons 2 and 7, not by Schedule C.

## Worked example

A trader who meets the Topic 429 test kept these 2025 records: market data $1,800; charting and scanning software $1,200; trade journal and tax-lot software $420; a continuing-education course on options pricing taken after two years of full-time trading $900; a 150-square-foot room used only for trading, deducted under the simplified method at $5 × 150 = $750; margin interest $2,600. Schedule C total: $1,800 + $1,200 + $420 + $900 + $750 + $2,600 = $7,670.

At a 24% marginal rate the Schedule C loss saves $7,670 × 0.24 = $1,840.80 of 2025 federal tax, and because the loss reduces adjusted gross income it can also reduce the base for the 3.8% NIIT and for state tax where applicable.

The same person as an investor: the $5,070 of data, software, education and home office is disallowed by section 67(g). The $2,600 of margin interest is investment interest on Form 4952, deductible only if the investor itemises; with no other itemised deductions the standard deduction of $15,750 is larger, so the deduction is worth $0 in 2025 and the interest carries forward under section 163(d)(2) until a year in which it can be used.

Had the education been the course that taught the trader to trade in the first place, taken in 2023 before the business existed, Reg. 1.162-5(b)(3) would remove the $900 from the trader's Schedule C as well.
