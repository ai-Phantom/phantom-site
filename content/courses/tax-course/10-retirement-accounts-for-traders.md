## Why the account matters more than the trade

Everything in lessons 1 to 9 applies to a taxable account. Inside an individual retirement account the questions those lessons answer mostly disappear. A traditional IRA is exempt from tax under section 408(e)(1); a Roth IRA under section 408A is treated the same way. Trades inside the account are not reported on Form 8949, there is no short-term versus long-term distinction, no wash-sale adjustment within the account, no $3,000 loss limit and no estimated tax on gains as they occur. Tax arrives, if at all, when money comes out. From a traditional IRA a distribution is ordinary income (plus a 10% additional tax before age 59½ unless an exception applies, Pub. 590-B). From a Roth IRA a qualified distribution, one made after the 5-year period that begins with the first year a Roth contribution was made and on or after age 59½, disability or death, is not taxable at all (Pub. 590-B, What Are Qualified Distributions?).

That is why active trading in a Roth IRA is uniquely efficient: a strategy whose gains are all short-term and would be taxed at up to 37% plus 3.8% in a taxable account compounds without tax drag and can be withdrawn tax-free. The price is that losses inside the account are worth nothing for tax purposes either, the contribution limits are small, and money cannot come out early without cost.

## Getting money in

Contributions require compensation. Publication 590-A defines compensation as what you earn from working, wages, salaries, tips, professional fees and net earnings from self-employment, and lists what it does not include, starting with earnings and profits from property. Trading gains are profits from property, and IRS Topic No. 429 says gains of a trader are not subject to self-employment tax, so they are not self-employment earnings either. A full-time trader with no wages and no other business has no compensation and cannot contribute to an IRA at all, even with a profitable year. A spouse with compensation can fund a spousal IRA on a joint return.

The annual limit is set by section 219(b)(5). Notice 2024-80 fixes it at $7,000 for 2025 with a $1,000 additional amount at age 50 or older; Notice 2025-67 raises it to $7,500 for 2026 with an $1,100 catch-up. Roth IRA contributions phase out with modified AGI under section 408A(c)(3): for 2025 between $150,000 and $165,000 for single filers and between $236,000 and $246,000 for joint filers; for 2026 between $153,000 and $168,000 and between $242,000 and $252,000 (Notice 2025-67 and IRS news release IR-2025-111).

## The solo 401(k) and why most traders cannot use it

A one-participant 401(k) lets a business owner with no employees contribute twice: as employee, elective deferrals up to $23,500 for 2025 and $24,500 for 2026 (section 402(g), Notices 2024-80 and 2025-67), plus catch-up of $7,500 (2025) or $8,000 (2026) at 50 or older and $11,250 at ages 60 to 63; and as employer, a profit-sharing contribution. The total, excluding catch-up, cannot exceed the section 415(c) limit of $70,000 for 2025 or $72,000 for 2026. The IRS page on one-participant 401(k) plans states that for a self-employed person compensation means earned income: net earnings from self-employment after deducting half the self-employment tax and the contributions themselves, computed with Publication 560's worksheets. A plan with $250,000 or more in assets at year end files Form 5500-EZ.

For a trader the conclusion follows from Topic 429: trading gains, with or without a section 475(f) election, are not net earnings from self-employment, so they cannot support a solo 401(k) contribution. Traders who have one fund it from other self-employment income, such as consulting, or through an entity structure that pays them compensation; both are outside this course and need a professional, because the plan rules, the reasonableness of compensation and the costs all have to be checked.

## Prohibited transactions

Section 4975 and Publication 590-B define a prohibited transaction as any improper use of the IRA by you, your beneficiary or a disqualified person (your fiduciary and your spouse, ancestors, lineal descendants and their spouses). Publication 590-B's examples include borrowing money from the IRA, selling property to it, using it as security for a loan and buying property for personal use with IRA funds. The consequence under section 408(e)(2)(A) is severe: the account ceases to be an IRA as of the first day of the year in which the transaction occurred, and Publication 590-B treats all its assets as distributed to you at fair market value on that day, taxable and possibly subject to the 10% additional tax. Pledging an IRA as collateral for a personal margin loan is the trader's version of this mistake.

## UBTI and margin

An IRA is exempt but pays tax on unrelated business taxable income. Section 512(b)(5) excludes gains from the sale of property, so ordinary trading gains in an IRA are not UBTI. Section 512(b)(4) overrides that exclusion for debt-financed property as defined in section 514: income and gain from property bought with acquisition indebtedness, which Publication 598 describes, is included in proportion to the debt. A margin loan used to buy securities inside an IRA is the standard way an IRA acquires such debt. Publication 590-B requires the IRA to file Form 990-T when it has $1,000 or more of gross unrelated business income, by April 15 for a calendar-year IRA, and the trustee may file on its behalf. Many IRA custodians offer limited margin, which settles trades without borrowing; whether a given account borrows is a question for the custodian's documents.

## The IRA wash-sale trap

Lesson 3 showed that Publication 550 lists a purchase of substantially identical stock in your IRA or Roth IRA as triggering a wash sale on a loss in your taxable account, and that the basis adjustment does not carry into the IRA. The loss is permanently lost. Automatic dividend reinvestment in an IRA holding the same fund you just harvested in a taxable account is the common way this happens.

## Worked example

Trade. You bought 40 TSLA on 2025-03-10 at the Yahoo Finance daily close of $222.15, cost $8,886.00, and sold them on 2025-05-27 at the close of $362.89, proceeds $14,515.60. Gain $5,629.60, held 78 days.

In a taxable account, single filer in the 24% bracket: short-term gain taxed at ordinary rates, $5,629.60 × 0.24 = $1,351.10 of 2025 federal tax, plus 3.8% NIIT ($213.92) if MAGI exceeds $200,000. In a traditional IRA: no 2025 tax; the $14,515.60 is taxed as ordinary income whenever it is withdrawn. In a Roth IRA: no 2025 tax and no tax on a later qualified distribution. Over a year of such trades the difference is the whole short-term tax bill.

Contribution check. You are single, under 50, with wages and 2025 modified AGI of $158,000. Roth contribution limit from Publication 590-A Worksheet 2-2: line 3, $158,000 − $150,000 = $8,000; line 5, $8,000 ÷ $15,000 = 0.533; line 7, $7,000 × 0.533 = $3,731.00; line 8, $7,000 − $3,731.00 = $3,269.00, rounded up to the next multiple of $10 = $3,270. Your 2025 Roth IRA contribution limit is $3,270, and the deadline for a 2025 contribution is the April 15, 2026 due date of the return. For 2026 the same MAGI gives ($158,000 − $153,000) ÷ $15,000 = 0.333; $7,500 × 0.333 = $2,497.50; $7,500 − $2,497.50 = $5,002.50, rounded up to $5,010.
