<p>The IRS does not treat all investment gains equally. Two investors who each make $50,000 trading the same stocks can owe dramatically different tax bills depending entirely on <em>how long</em> they held each position. Understanding this distinction is worth thousands of dollars a year.</p>
        <h2>Short-Term Capital Gains</h2>
        <p>Any asset held for <strong>one year or less</strong> before selling generates a short-term capital gain (or loss). Short-term gains are taxed at your ordinary income rate — the same rate as your salary. In 2025, this ranges from 10% to 37% depending on your total income. For a trader in the 32% bracket, a $10,000 short-term gain costs $3,200 in federal tax.</p>
        <h2>Long-Term Capital Gains</h2>
        <p>Assets held for <strong>more than one year</strong> qualify for preferential long-term capital gains rates: 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on income. For most individual investors, the applicable rate is 15%. That same $10,000 gain becomes a $1,500 tax bill instead of $3,200 — a $1,700 difference for simply waiting a few extra months.</p>
        <h2>The Math on Holding Period</h2>
        <p>Example: You bought NVDA in February at $600. By October (8 months later), it is at $800. You have a $200/share gain on 100 shares = $20,000 profit. At 32% ordinary rate = $6,400 federal tax. If you wait until the following February (12+ months): $20,000 × 15% = $3,000. <strong>Holding 4 more months saves $3,400.</strong> Of course, if you expect the stock to fall, the tax tail should not wag the investment dog — but when you are indifferent, the holding period decision is pure money.</p>
        <h2>Offsetting Gains with Losses</h2>
        <p>Capital losses offset capital gains dollar for dollar. Long-term losses offset long-term gains first, then short-term gains. Short-term losses offset short-term gains first. Up to $3,000 of net capital losses can offset ordinary income each year, with unlimited carryforward of any remaining losses.</p>
