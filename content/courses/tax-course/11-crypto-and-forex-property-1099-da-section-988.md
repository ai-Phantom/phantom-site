## Crypto is property

IRS Notice 2014-21, Q&A-1, says virtual currency is treated as property and that general tax principles applicable to property transactions apply. Publication 550 restates the rule for all digital assets, defined as a digital representation of value recorded on a cryptographically secured distributed ledger: they are property, the holding-period rules of lesson 1 apply, and the guidance list includes Notice 2023-34, Rev. Rul. 2019-24, Rev. Rul. 2023-14 and Rev. Proc. 2024-28. For an individual holding crypto as an investment, a disposal produces capital gain or loss: short-term if held one year or less, long-term if more, taxed exactly as stock gains are.

The property framing has consequences that surprise newcomers. Notice 2014-21, Q&A-6, says exchanging virtual currency for other property produces gain or loss, so trading one token for another is a taxable disposal of the first token at its fair market value in dollars, even though no dollars were involved. Spending crypto on goods is a disposal. Paying a network fee in crypto is a disposal of the coins used. Receiving crypto as payment, as a mining or staking reward, or in an airdrop is ordinary income at fair market value when received (Rev. Rul. 2023-14 for staking), and that value becomes the coins' basis. The IRS digital assets page sends such income to Schedule 1 and capital disposals to Form 8949.

Every Form 1040 also asks whether, at any time during the year, you received digital assets as a reward, award or payment, or sold, exchanged or otherwise disposed of a digital asset. Merely buying with dollars and holding is a No; any disposal is a Yes.

## Basis by wallet and the wash-sale question

Treasury Regulation 1.1012-1(j), applicable from 2025, requires basis to be identified for digital assets held in each wallet or account separately: specific identification is allowed if made in your records (unhosted wallets) or communicated to the broker (custodial accounts), and otherwise first-in, first-out applies wallet by wallet. Rev. Proc. 2024-28 gave a safe harbour under section 1012(c)(1) for allocating unused basis to wallets and accounts as of January 1, 2025, based on your records of that basis. A single universal FIFO pool across every exchange, the method many people used before 2025, is no longer the default.

IRC section 1091 applies to stock or securities. The guidance cited here treats digital assets as property, not securities, and does not apply section 1091 to them, which is why a crypto loss can generally be realised and the coin repurchased immediately. There is one exception visible in the forms: the 2026 Form 1099-DA instructions require brokers to report wash-sale losses on tokenized securities, digital assets that are themselves stock or securities, in box 1i. Whether a particular token is a security is a legal question this course does not answer.

## Form 1099-DA

Section 6045, as amended by the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act, extended broker reporting to digital assets. The IRS digital assets page says brokers must report gross proceeds on Form 1099-DA for transactions on or after January 1, 2025. The 2026 Form 1099-DA instructions add mandatory basis reporting for digital assets that are covered securities, defined as assets acquired after 2025 for cash or other property in an account where the broker provides custodial services and held there until sold. In practice your 2025 Forms 1099-DA show proceeds without basis, and you must supply basis from your own records. Assets transferred into an exchange from elsewhere are noncovered and stay that way. The Form 8949 instructions created boxes G, H and I (short-term) and J, K and L (long-term) for digital asset transactions and say not to use boxes A to F for them; a 2025 short-term sale reported on a 1099-DA without basis goes in Part I with box H checked.

## Forex: section 988 by default

Currency trading has its own section. IRC section 988 applies to foreign currency transactions including acquiring nonfunctional currency, and to forward contracts, futures contracts and options on currencies. Section 988(a)(1)(A) treats foreign currency gain or loss on a section 988 transaction as ordinary income or loss. For an individual trading spot currency with a retail dealer, the default is therefore ordinary treatment: gains taxed at ordinary rates, losses fully deductible against other income without the $3,000 capital loss cap. Section 988(e) excludes personal transactions, such as currency bought for travel, and section 988(e)(2) ignores gains of $200 or less on personal disposals of foreign currency.

Two routes lead out of ordinary treatment. Section 988(a)(1)(B) lets you elect capital treatment for a forward contract, futures contract or option that is a capital asset in your hands and not part of a straddle, but only if you make the election and identify the transaction before the close of the day you enter into it. Whether a rolling spot contract at a retail forex dealer qualifies as a forward contract for this election is not addressed in the sources cited here; confirm it with a CPA before relying on it. Second, section 988(c)(1)(D) removes from section 988 any regulated futures contract or nonequity option that would be marked to market under section 1256. A currency futures contract on the CME is therefore a section 1256 contract by default and gets the 60/40 treatment of lesson 5, unless you elect out of that exception under section 988(c)(1)(D)(ii) by the first day of the tax year. An interbank forward that qualifies as a foreign currency contract under section 1256(g)(2) is also a section 1256 contract.

## Worked example

Crypto. You bought 0.5 BTC on a custodial exchange on 2025-04-07 at the Yahoo Finance BTC-USD daily close of $79,235.34, cost $39,617.67, and sold it on 2025-10-06 at the close of $124,752.53, proceeds $62,376.27. Gain = $62,376.27 − $39,617.67 = $22,758.60. Holding period from 2025-04-08 to 2025-10-06 is under a year, so short-term. Tax at a 24% marginal rate: $22,758.60 × 0.24 = $5,462.06. Had you held until 2026-04-08 or later, the gain would have been long-term at 15% in the same bracket, before any price change.

Reporting. Your 2025 Form 1099-DA shows gross proceeds of $62,376.27 and no basis, because the coins were acquired before 2026 and are noncovered. On Form 8949, Part I, box H: (a) 0.5 BTC; (b) 04/07/2025; (c) 10/06/2025; (d) $62,376.27; (e) $39,617.67 from your records; (h) $22,758.60. Answer Yes to the digital asset question on Form 1040.

Forex. A trader with $150,000 of wages lost $12,000 trading spot EUR/USD with a retail dealer in 2025 and made no section 988(a)(1)(B) election. The loss is ordinary under section 988(a)(1)(A) and reduces taxable income by the full $12,000, worth $12,000 × 0.24 = $2,880 in the 24% bracket. Had the same $12,000 been a capital loss with no capital gains to absorb it, section 1211(b) would have allowed $3,000, worth $720, with $9,000 carried forward. Had it been a $12,000 gain, it would be taxed at 24% ($2,880) under section 988, but at the 18.6% blended rate ($2,232) if it had come from CME euro futures under section 1256.
