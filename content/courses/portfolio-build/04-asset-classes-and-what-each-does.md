## An asset class is a return driver

An asset class is a group of holdings whose returns come from the same underlying source. Stocks are paid by corporate profits. Bonds are paid by contractual interest and the return of principal. Cash is paid by short-term interest rates. Real estate is paid by rents. Commodities are paid by nobody; their return is entirely price change.

This matters because the previous lesson showed that correlation sets the floor on portfolio risk, and correlation comes from shared drivers. Two things paid by the same source will move together. So the way to get genuinely low correlation is to hold assets paid by different sources, which is what mixing asset classes does. Owning fifty stocks diversifies you across companies. Owning stocks and bonds diversifies you across drivers.

Below is what each class does, what it costs you in the bad years, and where the two things beginners most often ask about, options and crypto, fit.

## Stocks

Stocks are ownership claims on businesses. Over long periods they have returned more than any other liquid asset class: about 9.8% a year compounded for the S&P 500 from 1928 to 2023 in Aswath Damodaran's data, before inflation and costs. That return is compensation for two things: profits are uncertain, and stockholders are paid last.

The cost of the return is the drawdown. The S&P 500 fell about 37% in calendar 2008, 43% in 1931, and 18% in 2022. Peak to trough, it has lost half its value three times since 2000. Any allocation to stocks has to be made knowing that a 50% fall is a normal event over a multi-decade horizon, not a black swan.

Within stocks, the main sub-divisions are by geography (US, developed international, emerging), by size (large, mid, small) and by style (value, growth). Each carries a somewhat different mix of drivers, and the correlations between them sit around 0.7 to 0.9 rather than 1.0, so holding several helps a little. The cheapest way to hold all of them at once is a total-market fund: VTI covers roughly 3,500 US stocks at a 0.03% expense ratio, and VXUS covers about 8,000 non-US stocks at 0.05%, as of their 2025 prospectuses.

**Job in the portfolio:** long-run growth. **What it costs you:** the deepest drawdowns of any liquid class.

## Bonds

A bond is a loan. You hand over principal, the borrower pays interest on a schedule and returns the principal at maturity. Because the payments are contractual, a bond's return is far more predictable than a stock's, provided the borrower pays. US Treasuries carry essentially no default risk; corporate bonds carry some and pay extra yield for it.

The risk bonds do carry is interest-rate risk. When market rates rise, existing bonds with lower coupons become less valuable, and the longer the bond's remaining life, the larger the move. This is measured by duration: a fund with a duration of 6 years loses roughly 6% of its price for every one-point rise in yields. 2022 was the demonstration. The Federal Reserve raised rates faster than at any time since the early 1980s, the Bloomberg US Aggregate Bond Index fell 13.0%, and long-Treasury funds like TLT fell over 30%. Bonds are lower-risk than stocks; they are not risk-free, and they are least safe precisely when their duration is long.

The reason to hold them anyway is the correlation. Over most of 2000 to 2021, Treasury bonds rose when stocks fell, because the same recessions that hurt profits pushed rates down. In 2008 the Aggregate index returned +5.2% while the S&P 500 returned −37%. Even in 2022, when both fell, an intermediate bond fund fell less than stocks. BND, the Vanguard Total Bond Market ETF, holds about 11,000 investment-grade bonds with a duration near 6 years at 0.03%.

**Job in the portfolio:** cap the drawdown, provide something to sell to buy stocks after a crash. **What it costs you:** lower long-run return, and losses when rates rise fast.

## Cash

Cash means Treasury bills, money-market funds and high-yield savings, not a checking account. It has never had a negative nominal calendar year. Its return is whatever short rates are: near zero in 2021, over 5% in 2023.

The risk is inflation. In 2022 T-bills returned about 2% while consumer prices rose about 6.5%, so cash lost roughly 4.5% of its purchasing power. Over a decade of 3% inflation, a cash holding earning 1% loses about 18% of its real value without a single red day.

**Job in the portfolio:** money needed within about two years, and dry powder for rebalancing. **What it costs you:** guaranteed erosion by inflation if held too long. Funds like SGOV (0.09%) or BIL hold T-bills directly.

## Real estate, commodities and gold

Listed real estate (REITs) is paid by rents and carries stock-like volatility with a correlation to broad stocks around 0.6 to 0.7. It is a modest diversifier, and a total-market fund already includes it at roughly its market weight. Commodities have no cash flow; their return is price change plus the cost of rolling futures contracts, which has historically been a drag. Gold is the special case: no cash flow, but a long record of holding value in currency crises and low correlation with stocks. It also fell 28% in calendar 2013 and has gone two decades without a new high before. If you hold it, hold it small, and know that you are paying 0.25% to 0.40% a year (IAU, GLD) for an asset that pays nothing.

## Options: a tool, not an asset class

An option is a contract that gives its buyer the right, and its seller the obligation, to trade an underlying asset at a fixed price before an expiry date. It is not an asset class because it does not have its own return driver; it repackages the underlying's risk and adds a clock.

Two uses are legitimate in a portfolio context. Buying a put on an index fund is insurance against a fall; it costs a premium every time you renew it, and over long periods that premium is a persistent drag, so it is a choice about paying to limit drawdown, not a free lunch. Selling a covered call on a holding you own produces premium income in exchange for giving up gains above the strike; it caps your upside on that position and does nothing to protect the downside beyond the premium received.

Everything else, meaning buying calls or puts for directional bets, is trading, not portfolio construction, and belongs in the bounded satellite sleeve from lesson 6 or nowhere. The SEC's investor bulletin on options is the right place to learn the mechanics before you touch either use.

## Crypto: a high-volatility satellite at most

Crypto-assets have no cash flow and no agreed valuation model; their return is entirely what the next buyer pays. Bitcoin's annualised volatility has typically run 60% to 80%, three to four times that of the S&P 500, and its drawdowns have been of a different order: roughly −77% from the November 2021 peak near $69,000 to the November 2022 trough near $16,000, and about −83% in 2018. Correlation with stocks was near zero before 2020 and rose to around 0.4 to 0.6 in 2022, so it did not diversify a stock portfolio when that mattered.

The SEC's investor alerts on crypto-asset securities also flag risks that have nothing to do with price: exchange failures, custody loss and fraud. If you decide to hold any, the portfolio logic is the same as for any single high-volatility bet: a small fixed percentage, sized so that a 100% loss is survivable, and no rebalancing into it after a fall. Five percent of a portfolio going to zero is a bad year. Thirty percent going to zero is a changed life.

## Worked example

A 60/40 portfolio of SPY and AGG, using figures from 2010 to 2021: SPY annualised volatility about 18%, AGG about 5%, correlation about −0.1.

- w₁²σ₁² = 0.36 × 0.0324 = 0.011664
- w₂²σ₂² = 0.16 × 0.0025 = 0.000400
- Cross term: 2 × 0.6 × 0.4 × (−0.1) × 0.18 × 0.05 = −0.000432
- Variance: 0.011632
- Volatility: √0.011632 = 10.8%

The 40% bond sleeve took an 18% asset and produced a 10.8% portfolio: 60% of the stock exposure delivered 60% of the volatility, and the small negative correlation shaved a little more.

**Now the 2022 regime.** Correlation rose to about +0.5:

- Cross term: 2 × 0.6 × 0.4 × 0.5 × 0.18 × 0.05 = +0.002160
- Variance: 0.011664 + 0.000400 + 0.002160 = 0.014224
- Volatility: √0.014224 = 11.9%

**And the actual 2022 return:** 0.6 × (−18.1%) + 0.4 × (−13.0%) = −10.86% − 5.20% = −16.1%. Compare with 2008: 0.6 × (−37.0%) + 0.4 × (+5.2%) = −22.2% + 2.1% = −20.1%. The bond sleeve cut the 2008 loss by 17 points and the 2022 loss by only 2 points. Same allocation, different regime; the lesson is to expect the smaller benefit when sizing your risk budget, and treat the larger one as the good case.

## The rule that comes out of this

Hold the classes that are paid by something, in proportions that keep the worst plausible year inside your risk budget. Use the classes that are paid by nothing, and the tools that expire, in small, bounded amounts with a written reason, or not at all. The next lesson turns "small, bounded amounts" into a formula.
