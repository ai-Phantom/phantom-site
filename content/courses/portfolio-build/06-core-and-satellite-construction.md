## Two jobs, two sleeves

Everything so far has pointed the same way. Lesson 3 showed that a handful of stocks does not diversify you and that broad funds do. Lesson 4 showed that asset allocation, the split between stocks, bonds and cash, sets the drawdown. Lesson 5 showed that individual positions need a stop and a weight cap. Core-and-satellite is the structure that lets all three coexist in one account.

The **core** is the part of the portfolio that delivers the asset allocation. It holds broad, low-cost index funds in the proportions you decided in lesson 1, and it is left alone except to rebalance. It is where most of the money is, and it is boring by design.

The **satellite** sleeve is the part where you hold anything that is not the market: individual stocks, sector funds, a small gold or crypto position, a thematic bet. Each satellite has a written reason, a stop where relevant, and a weight cap. The sleeve as a whole has its own cap, typically 10% to 30% of the portfolio.

The split does two things at once. It guarantees that most of your money is diversified and cheap regardless of how the satellites turn out. And it gives you a bounded place to act on your own views, so that the urge to buy something does not end up reshaping the whole portfolio.

## Why the core should be index funds

Three lines of evidence, none of them controversial.

**Allocation explains most of the outcome.** Brinson, Hood and Beebower, studying 91 large US pension funds over 1974 to 1983, found that the funds' asset allocation policy explained more than 90% of the variation in their returns over time. Security selection and market timing explained the remainder and, on average, reduced returns. Later work argued about the precise figure and about what "explain" means, but nobody has found that stock picking dominates. Get the allocation right first.

**Most active managers underperform after costs.** S&P Dow Jones Indices publishes the SPIVA scorecard twice a year, comparing active funds with their benchmarks. In the scorecards covering periods ending 2023, roughly 88% of actively managed US large-cap funds had underperformed the S&P 500 over 15 years, and the figure is similar or worse in most other categories. These are professionals with research budgets. Holding the index in the core means you at least match the thing most of them fail to beat.

**Cost compounds against you.** The SEC's investor bulletin on fees shows the arithmetic: a 1% annual fee on a portfolio earning 4% a year reduces the ending balance by about 28% over 20 years. Index funds covering the whole US market now cost 0.03% a year. There is no cheaper way to own the return driver.

## What goes in the satellite sleeve

A satellite is any position you hold because you believe something the market price does not fully reflect. That belief has to be written down in a sentence you could later check: "Regional bank trades at 0.8x book value and I expect it to earn its way back to 1.2x within two years" is a thesis. "Great company" is not.

Sensible satellites for a first portfolio:

- Individual stocks, each sized by the lesson 5 rules and capped at 5% or so.
- A sector or factor fund, when you want tilt without single-name risk.
- A small gold or crypto position, if at all, capped where a total loss is a bad month.
- Cash held deliberately for a specific opportunity, with a date by which it goes back into the core if unused.

The sleeve cap matters as much as the per-position caps. Six satellites at 5% each is 30%, and if they are all technology stocks, lesson 3 says you have added one correlated 30% position, not six diversified ones. Check the sleeve's correlation with itself and with the core before you fill it.

## Worked example

A $50,000 portfolio, 80% core and 20% satellite, for a ten-year goal with a risk budget of roughly a 30% drawdown.

**Core: $40,000**

- VTI, US total stock market: 50%, $25,000, expense ratio 0.03%, annual cost 25,000 × 0.0003 = $7.50
- VXUS, non-US total stock market: 15%, $7,500, expense ratio 0.05%, annual cost 7,500 × 0.0005 = $3.75
- BND, US investment-grade bonds: 15%, $7,500, expense ratio 0.03%, annual cost 7,500 × 0.0003 = $2.25

Core annual fund cost: $7.50 + $3.75 + $2.25 = $13.50, a blended 0.034%.

**Satellites: $10,000**, four positions at 5% each, $2,500 apiece, each with a written thesis, a stop and the 5% cap.

**Compare the cost with an alternative.** The same $50,000 in an actively managed balanced fund at 0.70% costs $350 a year. Add a 1% advisory fee and the total is $850. Over 20 years at a 6% gross return, the difference between 0.03% and 1.7% in annual drag is:

- 50,000 × 1.0597^20 = 50,000 × 3.191 = $159,550
- 50,000 × 1.043^20 = 50,000 × 2.322 = $116,100

A $43,000 gap on a $50,000 start, from costs alone, before any difference in skill.

**Now stress a satellite.** One of the four positions loses 50%. Portfolio impact: 5% × 50% = 2.5%, or $1,250. Painful, survivable, and the other $48,750 is unaffected. Compare with a pile where that stock had drifted to 25% of the account: the same event costs 12.5%.

**And stress the core.** A 2008-type year: US stocks −37%, international stocks −43%, bonds +5%. Core return: 0.50 × (−37) + 0.15 × (−43) + 0.15 × (+5) = −18.5 − 6.45 + 0.75 = −24.2 points on the 80% core. If the satellites, being stocks, fall about 40%, that adds 0.20 × (−40) = −8.0 points. Portfolio: about −32%. That is right at the stated risk budget. If you could not hold through −32%, the fix is more bonds in the core, not fewer satellites, because the core is where the drawdown is set.

## Table

| | Core | Satellite |
|---|---|---|
| Share of portfolio | 70% to 90% | 10% to 30% |
| Holdings | Broad index funds, 3 to 5 of them | Individual stocks, sector funds, small alternatives |
| Reason to own | Deliver the asset allocation | A written thesis the market may not reflect |
| Cost | 0.03% to 0.10% | Varies; single stocks cost only the spread |
| Turnover | Rebalance only, once or twice a year | Bounded by stops and thesis reviews |
| Per-position cap | Set by allocation | 5% typical; never above 15% |
| What a 50% fall in one holding does | Impossible for a broad fund in one holding | −2.5% of portfolio at a 5% cap |
| Where the drawdown is set | Here, by the stock/bond split | Adds a few points at the sleeve cap |

## Deciding the split

The split between core and satellite is a statement about how much of your outcome you want to depend on your own judgement. At 90/10 the satellites are a hobby that cannot hurt you. At 70/30 they can move the result by several points a year in either direction. Above 70/30 you have a stock-picking portfolio with an index fund attached, and the evidence above says most people who run that arrangement would have done better without the picking.

A reasonable first setting is 80/20. Raise the satellite share only after a year in which your satellites, measured honestly by the method in lesson 11, beat the core they replaced. Lower it after a year in which they did not.
