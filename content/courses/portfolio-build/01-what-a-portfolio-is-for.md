## A pile is not a portfolio

Most first accounts are piles. You bought a stock a friend mentioned, then a fund your employer offered, then something that was going up, then a few shares of a company whose products you use. Each purchase made sense on its own. Together they answer no question, because none was asked.

A portfolio starts from the other end. You state what the money is for, when you need it, and how much of it you can afford to see disappear temporarily along the way. Then every position has to justify its place against those three constraints. If it cannot, it is not in the portfolio, however good the story.

That is the whole difference. Not the number of holdings, not whether you own funds or single names, not whether it is up this year. A portfolio is a set of holdings that was designed backwards from a goal. A pile is a set of holdings that was accumulated forwards from impulses.

This lesson gives you the three inputs, shows you how to turn a goal into a required return, and explains why the horizon, not the return, decides how much risk you may take.

## The three inputs

**Goal.** A goal is an amount and a date. "Retire comfortably" is a wish. "$60,000 by September 2036" is a goal. The amount lets you compute what return you need; the date tells you how long the money has to get there and how long it has to recover if something goes wrong.

**Horizon.** Horizon is the time between today and the date you start spending the money. It is the most important number in the whole exercise, because it determines whether a large fall is a temporary inconvenience or a permanent loss. Money that will be spent in two years cannot absorb a 40% fall. Money that will be spent in twenty years can, provided you do not sell at the bottom.

**Risk budget.** Risk budget is the largest temporary fall you can accept without abandoning the plan. Write it as a percentage and as a dollar figure, because they feel different. "25%" sounds tolerable in the abstract; "$15,000 of my $60,000 gone by Christmas" is what it looks like when it happens. If the dollar figure would make you sell, the percentage is too high.

Most people set the third number too high because they set it in a calm market. Set it as if you were already in the drawdown.

## Required return versus available return

Once you have an amount, a date and a starting balance, the goal implies a required rate of return. The market, meanwhile, offers whatever it offers. Your job is to see whether the two are compatible.

If the required return is low, you have room to take less risk than the market maximum, and you should consider doing so, because a lower-risk portfolio is one you are more likely to hold through a bad year. If the required return is higher than anything a sensible portfolio has delivered over long periods, the goal itself is wrong: you need to save more, extend the date, or reduce the amount. Taking more risk to close the gap is the one option that does not work reliably, because higher expected return comes bundled with a wider range of outcomes, including the outcome where you miss by more.

The long-run reference points, from Aswath Damodaran's compilation of annual returns 1928 to 2023, are roughly 9.8% a year compounded for the S&P 500 including dividends, about 4.9% for 10-year Treasury bonds and about 3.3% for 3-month Treasury bills, all before inflation and before costs. These are 96-year averages that contain a Depression, a war, a decade of double-digit inflation and three 50% crashes. They tell you what a very patient investor got, not what the next decade will pay.

## Worked example

Goal: $60,000 in 10 years for a house deposit. Starting balance $15,000. You can add $3,000 at the end of each year. What return does the plan require?

Future value of the starting balance plus the contributions:

FV = 15,000 × (1 + r)^10 + 3,000 × [((1 + r)^10 − 1) ÷ r]

Try r = 4%:

- (1.04)^10 = 1.4802
- Starting balance: 15,000 × 1.4802 = $22,203
- Contributions: 3,000 × [(1.4802 − 1) ÷ 0.04] = 3,000 × 12.006 = $36,018
- Total: $58,221. Short by about $1,800.

Try r = 5%:

- (1.05)^10 = 1.6289
- Starting balance: 15,000 × 1.6289 = $24,433
- Contributions: 3,000 × [(1.6289 − 1) ÷ 0.05] = 3,000 × 12.578 = $37,734
- Total: $62,167. Over by about $2,200.

The target sits between the two, at roughly 4.5% a year.

Now compare with the reference points. A 4.5% requirement is below the historical bond return and far below the stock return. The goal does not force you into an all-stock portfolio. A mix that historically returned 6% or so with much smaller falls than stocks alone would have got you there with margin. That margin is worth having, because the 10-year figure above assumes the return arrives smoothly, and it will not.

Now change one input. Suppose the deposit is needed in 3 years, not 10, and you have $45,000 today. The required return is (60,000 ÷ 45,000)^(1/3) − 1 = (1.333)^(0.333) − 1 = 10.1% a year, for three years running. That is above the long-run stock average with no room for a bad year, and a bad year within a three-year window is common. This goal is not compatible with a safe plan. The honest answers are: save more, buy a cheaper house, or wait. Loading up on risk is how people arrive at the closing date with $38,000.

## Why the horizon decides

Think about what a 35% fall does to each of the two plans above.

In the 10-year plan, a 35% fall in year 3 takes a balance of roughly $26,000 down to about $17,000. Ugly, but you have seven years and $21,000 of further contributions ahead of you, and those contributions buy at lower prices. The plan survives if you do.

In the 3-year plan, a 35% fall in year 2 takes $50,000 to $32,500 with one year left. No plausible return closes that gap. The plan is dead, and the only thing that would have saved it is not having been exposed to the fall in the first place.

Same event, same percentage, opposite outcomes. The difference is entirely the horizon. This is why the standard advice ties equity exposure to time: not because stocks are guaranteed to recover within any particular window, but because the longer the window, the more likely recovery is to arrive before the money is needed, and the more contributions you will make at depressed prices along the way.

## Table

A simple way to hold this in your head is to split money by when it is needed and give each bucket a different job.

| Horizon | Job of the money | Tolerable temporary fall | Typical building blocks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 to 2 years | Be there, in full, on the date | Near zero | Treasury bills, money market funds, high-yield savings |
| 2 to 5 years | Beat inflation without a large loss | 10% to 15% | Short and intermediate bond funds, some broad equity |
| 5 to 10 years | Grow, with time to recover from one bad cycle | 20% to 30% | Balanced mix of broad equity and bond funds |
| 10+ years | Compound; volatility is the price of return | 40% or more | Mostly broad equity funds, small bond allocation |

The percentages are ranges, not rules. The point of the table is the structure: the same person can sensibly hold near-cash for next year's tuition and a mostly-equity portfolio for retirement, because those are two different goals with two different horizons. Mixing them into one account with one allocation gets both wrong.

## What goes in the plan, and what does not

A position belongs in a portfolio when you can say, in one sentence, what job it does toward the goal. "Broad US equity exposure for long-run growth." "Bonds to cap the drawdown at a level I can hold through." "Cash for the deposit due in eighteen months." If the only sentence you can write is "I think it will go up," it is a trade, and trades live in a separate, bounded part of the account or not at all.

This does not mean single stocks are forbidden. It means they need a stated reason and a size limit, which is what lessons 5 through 7 cover.

## Before the next lesson

Write your goal as three numbers: an amount, a date and a maximum tolerable temporary fall in both percent and dollars. Then compute the required return using the formula above. If it comes out above 8% or so, revisit the inputs before you go any further, because the rest of this course assumes a goal that is reachable without heroics.
