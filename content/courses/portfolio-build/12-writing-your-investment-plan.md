## The document that runs the portfolio

Everything in this course has produced a decision: a goal and risk budget, an allocation, a core, a satellite sleeve with caps, sizing rules, rebalancing bands, drawdown responses, a measurement method. Left in your head, those decisions are available for renegotiation the first time the market makes them uncomfortable. Written down, dated and signed, they are a plan.

Professionals call this an investment policy statement, and the CFA Institute publishes a template for individual investors. Yours does not need to be long. Two pages is typical; one is fine. What it needs is to be specific enough that on any given day the question "what do I do?" has an answer you can look up.

This lesson gives you the twelve sections, a worked example with numbers, and the rules for changing the document.

## The twelve sections

**1. Purpose.** One paragraph: what the money is for and what this document is for. "This portfolio funds a house deposit in September 2036. This document records the rules I will follow so that I do not change them under stress."

**2. Goals.** Amount, date, required return (lesson 1). If there is more than one goal with different horizons, list each and state whether it has its own account.

**3. Horizon.** Years to the first withdrawal and, if relevant, the withdrawal schedule after that.

**4. Risk budget.** Maximum tolerable temporary fall, in percent and in dollars at the current balance, set as if you were in it. And the stress test: what the plan's allocation is expected to lose in a 2008-type year, which must be inside the budget.

**5. Constraints.** Money that must be available by a date. Tax situation: which accounts are taxable, which are sheltered, your marginal rate. Anything you will not hold on principle. Any employer stock or restricted holdings you cannot sell.

**6. Target allocation with bands.** The stock/bond/cash split, the funds that implement it, and the rebalancing band for each (lesson 8).

**7. Core and satellite rules.** The sleeve cap, the per-position cap, the look-through limit by name and sector (lessons 6 and 7).

**8. Sizing and exit rules.** Risk per position, the stop requirement for every satellite, the thesis review date requirement, the 48-hour rule for new purchases (lessons 5 and 10).

**9. Rebalancing schedule.** The two check dates, the order of levers (contributions, dividends, reserve, then sales), the lot-selection rule for taxable sales.

**10. Drawdown response.** What you will do at −15%, −25% and −35%. Usually: "rebalance per section 9; nothing else."

**11. Measurement.** The benchmark blend, the seven numbers to record annually, the date of the annual review (lesson 11).

**12. What I will not do.** The foreseeable temptations, as prohibitions: no margin, no options beyond covered calls on core holdings, no crypto above 3%, no purchase without a written thesis, no allocation change within 30 days of a ±10% market move, no checking the account more than weekly outside the rebalancing dates.

Then a signature and a date, and a line for the date of the next scheduled review.

## Worked example

A plan for a $30,000 portfolio with $500 monthly contributions, a ten-year horizon and a first draft of a 70/30 allocation.

**Section 4, risk budget.** The investor decides the largest fall they could hold through is 30%, which at $30,000 is $9,000. Now the stress test. Using the stress values from lesson 4, −50% for stocks (a peak-to-trough figure, harsher than the 2008 calendar year) and −10% for bonds (2022 was −13% for the Aggregate, so −10% is a reasonable round figure for an intermediate fund):

Expected drawdown at 70/30 = 0.70 × (−50) + 0.30 × (−10) = −35 − 3 = −38%

In dollars: 0.38 × 30,000 = $11,400. That is $2,400 beyond the budget. The plan fails its own test before it is signed.

**Fix the allocation, not the budget.** Try 60/40:

0.60 × (−50) + 0.40 × (−10) = −30 − 4 = −34%. Still over.

Try 55/45:

0.55 × (−50) + 0.45 × (−10) = −27.5 − 4.5 = −32%. Still over, marginally.

Try 50/50:

0.50 × (−50) + 0.50 × (−10) = −25 − 5 = −30%. On the budget.

So the allocation is 50/50, not 70/30. The investor's instinct was to take more risk than their own budget permitted, and the arithmetic caught it.

**Section 2, does 50/50 still reach the goal?** Suppose the goal is $110,000 in ten years. With $30,000 start and $6,000 a year of contributions, the required return is found as in lesson 1:

At 5%: 30,000 × 1.6289 + 6,000 × 12.578 = 48,867 + 75,468 = $124,335.

At 4%: 30,000 × 1.4802 + 6,000 × 12.006 = 44,406 + 72,036 = $116,442.

At 3%: 30,000 × 1.3439 + 6,000 × 11.464 = 40,317 + 68,784 = $109,101.

The goal needs just over 3% a year. A 50/50 portfolio has historically returned well above that, so the lower-risk allocation costs nothing the goal needs. If the goal had required 8%, the investor would have had a real conflict, and the honest resolutions are the ones from lesson 1: more savings, a later date, or a smaller target.

**Section 6, the allocation with bands.** VTI 35% (band 30 to 40), VXUS 15% (12 to 18), BND 40% (35 to 45), SGOV 10% (7 to 13). Satellite sleeve up to 20% carved from the VTI weight, so if fully used: VTI 15%, satellites 20%.

**Section 8, sizing.** Risk per satellite 1% of portfolio, so $300 at the current balance. Per-position cap 5%. A satellite at the cap ($1,500) with a 20% stop risks $300, exactly the rule.

**Section 10, drawdown response.** "At −15%: check bands on the next scheduled date; do nothing before it. At −25%: rebalance to target immediately using SGOV and contributions, then sales if needed. At −35%: same as −25%; in addition, re-read section 4 and note in the log that this is inside the plan."

## Changing the plan

The plan changes when your life changes: a new goal, a changed date, a different income, a marriage, an inheritance, a job loss. It does not change because the market moved. The annual review is the scheduled moment to revisit every section with the previous year's seven numbers in front of you. Between reviews, the only permitted amendment is one triggered by a change in section 2, 3 or 5.

Keep the old versions. A plan that has been through three annual reviews, each dated, with the reasons for each change noted, is a record of how your thinking developed, and it is the best protection against the belief, which will arrive, that you have always known what you know now.

## Sign it

A plan you have not signed is a draft. Sign and date it, write the next review date, and put a copy where you will see it before you can place an order. Then, in the capstone, you will build a portfolio against it and try to break it.
