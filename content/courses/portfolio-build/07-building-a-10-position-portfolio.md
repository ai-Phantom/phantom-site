## The build, in order

This lesson assembles everything into a concrete ten-position portfolio. The order matters. Beginners pick stocks first and discover the allocation afterwards; you will do it the other way round, because lesson 6 showed that allocation is where the result is decided.

The eight steps:

1. Fix the amount, the horizon and the risk budget (lesson 1).
2. Set the stock/bond/cash split that keeps the worst plausible year inside the budget (lesson 4).
3. Fill the core with three or four broad funds (lesson 6).
4. Choose satellites, each with a written thesis and a sector label.
5. Size every position: fixed-fractional for the core, risk-per-trade plus weight cap for satellites (lesson 5).
6. Check overlap and correlation, including look-through (lesson 3).
7. Decide how to enter: at once or in stages.
8. Record everything in one table.

Ten positions is the target because it is enough to hold a diversified core and five bounded satellites, and few enough that you can know every line. A first portfolio with forty positions is not more diversified; it is less understood.

## Step 1 and 2: the budget and the split

Take a $20,000 portfolio, a ten-year horizon and a risk budget of a 30% temporary fall, which is $6,000. Lesson 4's arithmetic says a portfolio that is roughly 75% stocks and 25% bonds and cash lost about 25% to 30% in 2008-type conditions once satellites are included, so that is the split.

## Step 3: the core

Sixty-five percent of the portfolio, four funds:

- VTI, US total market, 30%.
- VXUS, international total market, 15%.
- BND, US investment-grade bonds, 15%.
- SGOV, Treasury bills, 5%. This is the rebalancing reserve and the place new contributions wait until the next rebalance date.

The core alone is already a complete portfolio. If you stopped here you would hold about 11,000 stocks and 11,000 bonds for a blended cost near 0.04% a year. Everything that follows is optional.

## Step 4: the satellites

Thirty-five percent in five positions at 7% each. The selection rule for a first portfolio is: five different sectors, each with a thesis you can write in one line and check in a year. For the example, using large, liquid names so that spreads and liquidity are not a concern:

- JPM (financials): scale advantage in US banking; thesis is return on equity above peers persisting.
- UNH (health care): managed-care scale; thesis is earnings growth in line with guidance.
- XOM (energy): low-cost production and buybacks; thesis is free cash flow above dividends at $70 oil.
- COST (consumer staples): membership model; thesis is renewal rates above 90% and steady comparable-store growth.
- MSFT (technology): cloud share gains; thesis is Azure growth above 25% a year.

These are examples of the form a satellite list should take, not recommendations. Your five will be different, and they should be, because the thesis has to be one you hold.

## Step 5: sizing

The core is fixed-fractional: the percentages above, in dollars.

Each satellite is 7% of $20,000, or $1,400. That is the weight cap. Now check it against the risk-per-trade rule. Suppose the account's per-position risk is 1.5%, or $300. A $1,400 position with a stop 20% below entry risks $280, inside the rule. A stop 30% below entry would risk $420, outside the rule; the fix is either a tighter stop, if the thesis supports one, or a smaller position of $1,000. Take the smaller of the two numbers, always.

## Step 6: overlap and correlation

The five satellites are in five sectors, so their pairwise correlations are lower than five technology stocks would be, roughly 0.3 to 0.5 for large caps over 2019 to 2023 rather than 0.6. But every one of them is also inside VTI. The check that matters is look-through concentration: how much of the portfolio is in each company once the fund holdings are counted.

## Worked example

$20,000 portfolio. Assumed prices are round numbers for arithmetic; use live quotes when you build your own.

Weight, dollars, assumed price, shares, and approximate annualised volatility for each line:

1. VTI, core, broad US: 30%, $6,000, $300, 20 shares, 17%
2. VXUS, core, broad ex-US: 15%, $3,000, $65, 46 shares, 17%
3. BND, core, bonds: 15%, $3,000, $75, 40 shares, 5%
4. SGOV, core, T-bills: 5%, $1,000, $100, 10 shares, 0.5%
5. JPM, satellite, financials: 7%, $1,400, $250, 5.6 shares, 25%
6. UNH, satellite, health care: 7%, $1,400, $350, 4 shares, 25%
7. XOM, satellite, energy: 7%, $1,400, $110, 12.7 shares, 25%
8. COST, satellite, consumer staples: 7%, $1,400, $900, 1.6 shares, 20%
9. MSFT, satellite, technology: 7%, $1,400, $450, 3.1 shares, 25%
10. Cash reserve, core: 0% at build, the destination for rounding remainders and dividends

Fractional shares are shown; if your broker does not support them, round down and leave the remainder in SGOV.

**Look-through on Microsoft.** Microsoft's weight in VTI was around 6% in 2024 to 2025 fund reports (check the current holdings page). Through VTI: 30% × 6% = 1.8%. Direct: 7%. Total exposure to one company: 8.8%. That is inside a 10% single-name look-through limit but it is worth knowing, because the direct MSFT position is not adding a new bet; it is increasing an existing one. The same check on JPM (about 1.3% of VTI) gives 0.4% + 7% = 7.4%.

**Look-through on technology as a sector.** Technology was roughly 30% of VTI in that period, so the core contributes 30% × 30% = 9%, plus the 7% in MSFT: about 16% of the portfolio in one sector. Reasonable, but if two more satellites were technology stocks the figure would be 30% and lesson 3's correlation floor would apply.

**A simple risk number.** The weighted average volatility ignores diversification, so it is an upper bound and easy to compute:

0.30 × 17 + 0.15 × 17 + 0.15 × 5 + 0.05 × 0.5 + 0.07 × (25 + 25 + 25 + 20 + 25)
= 5.10 + 2.55 + 0.75 + 0.03 + 0.07 × 120
= 5.10 + 2.55 + 0.75 + 0.03 + 8.40
= 16.8%

True portfolio volatility, with correlations below 1, would be around 13% to 14%. Either figure is fine as a first risk number; what matters is that it is written down and compared with the same number next year.

**The drawdown check.** Apply a 2008-type year: US stocks −37%, international −43%, bonds +5%, T-bills +2%, satellites −40% on average.

0.30 × (−37) + 0.15 × (−43) + 0.15 × 5 + 0.05 × 2 + 0.35 × (−40)
= −11.1 − 6.45 + 0.75 + 0.1 − 14.0
= −30.7%

That is $6,140 on $20,000, right at the risk budget. If that number would make you sell, move 10 points from VTI to BND now, while nothing is wrong. The check is cheap; the regret is not.

## Step 7: entering

Vanguard's research on the question, most recently in 2023, found that investing a lump sum immediately beat spreading it over twelve months about 68% of the time across historical periods, because markets rise more often than they fall. The expected cost of waiting is modest, a fraction of a percent on average, and the benefit is behavioural: someone who would otherwise sit in cash for a year out of fear does better by averaging in over three months than by not investing.

The practical rule: enter the core at once, or over no more than three months if the idea of a bad first month would make you abandon the plan. Enter satellites one at a time as each thesis is written, not all on the same day.

## Step 8: the record

Every position gets one line: ticker, sleeve, weight, dollars, shares, entry date, entry price, stop (satellites only), and the one-sentence reason. This record is what you rebalance against (lesson 8), measure against (lesson 11), and consult when a red day tempts you to change something (lesson 10). A portfolio without the record is a pile with better intentions.
