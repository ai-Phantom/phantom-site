## The gap between the portfolio and its owner

A fund can return 7.3% a year while the people invested in it earn 6.3%. Morningstar's "Mind the Gap" study measures exactly this, comparing funds' reported time-weighted returns with the dollar-weighted returns their investors actually received, and for the ten years to the end of 2023 the gap was about 1.1 percentage points a year. The funds did not lose the money. The investors did, by buying after rises and selling after falls.

That gap is the cost of behaviour, and it is larger than the fee difference between a cheap fund and an expensive one. This lesson names the mistakes that cause it, cites the evidence that they are systematic rather than personal failings, and gives you the only defence that has been shown to work: decide in advance, in writing, and follow the writing.

## The mistakes

**Loss aversion.** Kahneman and Tversky's 1979 prospect theory paper established that losses are felt roughly twice as strongly as equivalent gains. A $1,000 loss hurts about as much as a $2,000 gain pleases. This is why a portfolio down 15% feels like a crisis and one up 15% feels like nothing much, and why the urge to "do something" arrives with the losses.

**The disposition effect.** Terrance Odean, using trading records from 10,000 brokerage accounts, found in 1998 that investors sold winning positions at a far higher rate than losing ones. Selling a winner locks in a gain, which feels good; selling a loser admits a mistake, which does not. The losers they held then underperformed the winners they sold over the following year. The behaviour was both common and costly.

**Overtrading.** Barber and Odean's 2000 study of 66,000 households found that the most active fifth turned over their portfolios about 250% a year and earned 11.4% annually against 17.9% for the market and 16.4% for the average household. Their picks were not unusually bad; the costs of trading them were. The paper's title is "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth," and the mechanism was overconfidence: the belief that activity would add value when it only added cost.

**Recency.** The last twelve months feel like the future. After a strong year, the risk budget from lesson 1 seems too cautious; after a bad one, too aggressive. Both feelings are the same error: weighting recent data far above its statistical importance.

**Anchoring.** The purchase price becomes a reference point that has no bearing on the holding's value. "I'll sell when it gets back to what I paid" is anchoring; the market does not know or care what you paid.

**Herding.** Buying what is being talked about. The information has already been priced by the time it is a conversation, and the purchase is a bet that the last buyer will not be you.

**Action bias.** In a drawdown, doing nothing feels irresponsible even when it is the plan. The rebalancing rule is the only "something" that is justified, and it is usually the opposite of what action bias suggests.

## Why willpower does not work

Every one of the mistakes above is strongest exactly when the decision is being made. Loss aversion peaks in the drawdown; recency peaks after the run; herding peaks when everyone is talking. Telling yourself you will be rational at that moment is a plan to fight your own psychology at its strongest point.

The alternative is to move the decision. Make it now, in calm conditions, with the arithmetic from lessons 5, 8 and 9 in front of you, write it down, and treat the written rule as the decision. At the moment of stress, your only job is to follow it. This is pre-commitment, and it is the reason the lessons on sizing and rebalancing insisted on written stops, written bands and written dates: a rule that exists only in your head is available for renegotiation when it is least convenient.

## Worked example

The cost of the behaviour gap, in dollars. Suppose you contribute $10,000 at the end of each year for 20 years. The portfolio itself returns 7% a year. Because of the timing of your own buying and selling, you earn 5.9%, a gap of 1.1 points, the Morningstar figure.

**At 7%:**

- (1.07)^20 = 3.8697
- Future value of the contributions: 10,000 × [(3.8697 − 1) ÷ 0.07] = 10,000 × 40.995 = $409,955

**At 5.9%:**

- (1.059)^20 = 3.1471
- Future value: 10,000 × [(3.1471 − 1) ÷ 0.059] = 10,000 × 36.392 = $363,920

**Gap:** 409,955 − 363,920 = $46,035, about 11% of the final balance and more than four and a half years of contributions.

**Now the disposition effect on one position.** You hold a satellite bought at $100 with a written stop at $80. It falls to $80. The rule says sell, taking a $20 per-share loss. Anchoring and loss aversion say hold, because "it will come back." Odean's data says the held loser underperforms. Suppose it drifts to $60 over the next year while the position you would have moved into rose 10%. The cost of ignoring the stop, per $1,000 originally invested: the rule leaves you with $800 × 1.10 = $880; holding leaves you with $600. The difference is $280, or 28% of the original position, on a single instance of not following a rule you had already written.

## Table

| Bias | What it feels like | What it makes you do | Pre-commitment rule that blocks it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loss aversion | Panic at −15% | Sell into the drawdown | Written drawdown response: "At −15%, −25%, −35%: rebalance per the band rule; nothing else" |
| Disposition effect | Relief at selling a winner; hope on a loser | Sell winners early, hold losers | Every satellite has a stop and a thesis-review date; sell on either, regardless of P&L |
| Overtrading | Confidence; boredom | Turnover, spread costs | Two rebalancing dates a year; satellite trades only on stop, thesis break or scheduled review |
| Recency | "This time the trend is clear" | Raise risk after a run, cut it after a fall | Allocation changes only at the annual plan review, never within 30 days of a ±10% move |
| Anchoring | "Just wait until it gets back to my price" | Hold past the stop | Stops are set on the thesis, not on cost basis; cost basis is not written on the position sheet |
| Herding | Everyone is buying it | Add a satellite with no thesis | No purchase without a one-line thesis written 48 hours before the order |
| Action bias | Doing nothing feels negligent | Trade for its own sake | A "do nothing" line in the plan, and a log of what you did not do |

## Writing rules that hold

A pre-commitment rule has three parts: a trigger you can observe without judgement, an action that requires no judgement, and no escape clause.

Weak: "If the market gets really bad I'll rebalance." Which market, how bad, rebalance to what?

Strong: "On 30 June and 31 December, if VTI's weight is above 35% or below 25%, sell or buy to return it to 30%, using SGOV first."

Weak: "I'll cut losers." Strong: "JPM: stop at $215. If it closes below $215, sell the full position the next trading day. Thesis review 15 March; if return on equity is below 14% in the latest annual report, sell regardless of price."

The 48-hour rule for new purchases is worth singling out. Almost every regretted purchase in a first portfolio was made within an hour of the idea arriving. Requiring the thesis to be written and left overnight removes most of them at zero cost; if the idea is good, it will still be good on Thursday.

Put the rules where you will see them before you can act: the first page of the plan document from lesson 12, or a note pinned above the broker login. The point is not that the rules are clever. The point is that at the moment they are needed, you will not be.
