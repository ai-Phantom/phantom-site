<p>Every investment decision is ultimately a tradeoff between two variables: the expected return and the risk taken to achieve it. The goal of portfolio risk management is not to eliminate risk — that would eliminate all return. The goal is to ensure you are being adequately compensated for every unit of risk you take.</p>
        <h2>Risk-Adjusted Return — The Only Number That Matters</h2>
        <p>A 40% annual return sounds extraordinary. But if you achieved it by concentrating 80% of your portfolio in a single speculative biotech stock — taking on volatility 4× the market — you took massive risk for a return you might have achieved with far less. A 20% return generated with lower volatility than the S&P 500 is objectively a better outcome on a risk-adjusted basis.</p>
        <h2>The Sharpe Ratio</h2>
        <p>The Sharpe ratio measures return per unit of risk: (Portfolio Return − Risk-Free Rate) ÷ Standard Deviation of Returns. The higher, the better. As a benchmark: a Sharpe ratio of 0.5 is mediocre, 1.0 is good, 1.5 is excellent, 2.0+ is rare and exceptional. The S&P 500 has historically averaged a Sharpe of approximately 0.5–0.7 over long periods.</p>
        <h2>Why Most Retail Portfolios Fail on This Metric</h2>
        <p>Retail portfolios typically have poor Sharpe ratios not because returns are low, but because volatility is unnecessarily high. Concentration in a handful of high-beta tech stocks produces violent drawdowns — 40–60% peak-to-trough in bear markets — that most investors cannot psychologically endure, causing them to sell at the worst possible time.</p>
