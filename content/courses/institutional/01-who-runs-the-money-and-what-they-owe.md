## Start with the liability, not the assets

Every professional portfolio exists to pay someone. A pension pays retirees. An endowment pays a university's budget. An insurer pays claims. A sovereign fund pays a government. A mutual fund pays whoever redeems tomorrow. The single most useful institutional habit, and the one that scales down perfectly, is to name that obligation before you name a single holding. The obligation determines the horizon, the liquidity you need, the risk you can carry, and what "a bad year" even means.

Individual investors usually start at the other end. They start with an idea ("I like semiconductors") or a product ("my broker offers this fund") and back into a portfolio. Institutions cannot do that, because a committee will ask the same question every quarter: relative to what we owe, are we on track? This lesson walks through the main institution types, what each one owes, and how that debt shows up in real allocations and real numbers.

## Six kinds of institutional money

**Defined-benefit pension plans** promise a formula-based income for life. The liability is a stream of payments stretching decades ahead, discounted to a present value at an assumed rate. CalPERS, the largest US public plan, uses a 6.8% discount rate. If the assets earn less than that for long enough, contributions from employers (ultimately taxpayers) must rise. The plan's risk is therefore two-sided: too little return raises contributions; too much volatility raises the chance of a forced contribution spike after a bad year. Pensions hold a mix of public equity, private equity, fixed income, real assets and increasingly private credit, and many of them run modest leverage at the total-fund level.

**Endowments and foundations** owe a perpetual spending stream, usually 4–5.5% of a smoothed asset value, plus the requirement to preserve purchasing power forever. There is no dated claim, no regulator demanding a capital ratio, and no redemption window. That is why Yale, Harvard and their peers can carry more illiquid assets than anyone else. Harvard's FY2025 report frames the target simply: roughly 5% distributions plus roughly 3% inflation gives an 8% long-term return objective. Yale distributed $2.0 billion to the operating budget in fiscal 2024 from a $41.4 billion endowment, which is 4.83% of the ending value.

**Insurers** owe claims. A property-casualty insurer's claims arrive within a few years; a life insurer's arrive over decades. In both cases the liabilities are contractual, can be estimated actuarially, and are sensitive to interest rates, so insurers hold mainly investment-grade bonds whose duration is matched to the liabilities and are regulated on risk-based capital. Equities are a small, deliberate slice.

**Sovereign wealth funds** vary, but the largest, Norway's Government Pension Fund Global, has a clear mandate: invest the country's oil revenue abroad and support the budget under a fiscal rule that limits withdrawals to the fund's expected real return, currently set at 3%. At the end of 2024 the fund held 19,742 billion kroner, 71.4% in equities, 26.6% in fixed income, 1.8% in unlisted real estate and 0.1% in unlisted renewable infrastructure, against a benchmark built around a 70% equity share. It reported a 13.1% return for 2024 and management costs of 0.04% of assets.

**Mutual funds** owe their own shareholders the net asset value on any business day. The Investment Company Act of 1940 requires daily redemption at NAV, so a fund's real liability is liquidity: it must be able to sell without moving the price against the remaining holders. That, plus the prospectus mandate ("US large-cap growth"), is what a mutual fund manager is actually managing.

**Hedge funds** owe limited partners the ability to withdraw at set windows (monthly, quarterly, or annually with notice), under a partnership agreement rather than a statute. Their liability is contractual liquidity plus a performance-fee contract with high-water marks, which you will study in Lesson 7.

## What the liability decides

Look at how the obligation drives the answers to four questions.

*Horizon.* An endowment's horizon is infinite, so it can hold 10-year private-equity partnerships. A property-casualty insurer's horizon is the average claim lag, so it holds 3–5 year bonds.

*Liquidity.* A mutual fund can be asked for cash tomorrow; a pension knows its benefit payments a year ahead; an endowment knows its distribution formula years ahead. The more predictable the outflow, the more illiquidity the portfolio can afford.

*Risk definition.* For a pension, risk is falling funded status. For an endowment, risk is a real spending cut. For an insurer, risk is a regulatory capital shortfall. For a mutual fund, risk is underperforming the benchmark in the prospectus. "Volatility" is a proxy that means something different in each case.

*Required return.* This is the number the whole portfolio is built to hit, and it comes straight from the liability: spend rate plus inflation for an endowment, discount rate for a pension, guaranteed crediting rates for an insurer.

## Worked example

Take three real balance sheets as of their latest reports and work out what each one owes.

**CalPERS, June 30, 2024.** Assets: $502.9 billion. Estimated funded status: 75%. Discount rate: 6.8%. FY2023-24 net return: 9.3%.

Liabilities are assets divided by funded status: $502.9B / 0.75 = $670.5B. The unfunded liability is $670.5B - $502.9B = $167.6B. The 9.3% return beat the 6.8% discount rate by 2.5 percentage points; on $502.9B of ending assets that gap is worth roughly 0.025 × $460B of average assets ≈ $11.5B of "extra" funding in one year, which is why a single good year moved the funded ratio up but nowhere near to 100%. The reported 10-year annualized return was 6.2%, below the 6.8% discount rate: over a decade the assets have grown slower than the liabilities were assumed to. That is what an unfunded pension looks like in numbers, and it is why CalPERS has raised its private-market allocations in search of return rather than cut risk.

**Yale, June 30, 2024.** Endowment: $41.4 billion, up from $40.7 billion. Return: 5.7% net of fees, or $2.3 billion of gains. Distributions to the budget: $2.0 billion. Gifts: $231 million.

Check the arithmetic: $40.7B + $2.3B - $2.0B + $0.231B = $41.2B, within rounding of the reported $41.4B (the difference is other transfers). Spend rate on ending value: $2.0B / $41.4B = 4.83%. The required nominal return to hold real value is spend plus inflation. Using J.P. Morgan's 2025 long-term inflation assumption of 2.4%: 4.83% + 2.4% ≈ 7.2%. Yale's 5.7% fell short of that in fiscal 2024, but its 10-year annualized return of 9.5% and 20-year return of 10.3% exceeded it comfortably; over the decade it distributed $14.3 billion. An endowment is judged on the decade, not the year, precisely because its liability is perpetual.

**Norway, December 31, 2024.** Fund value: 19,742 billion kroner. Return: 13.1%, or 2,511 billion kroner. Management cost: 0.04% of assets.

Under the fiscal rule the government can withdraw about 3% of the fund's value: 19,742 × 0.03 = 592 billion kroner a year. Management cost: 19,742 × 0.0004 = 7.9 billion kroner (the fund reported 7.4 billion, the difference being average rather than year-end assets). Set the two side by side and you see the design: the fund's job is to deliver a 3% real return at almost no cost, with 70% equity risk and a benchmark it tracks tightly, not to find alpha.

Three institutions, three liabilities, three completely different definitions of success: funded status, real spending power, and a cheap real return on a benchmark. None of them starts with a stock pick.

## What scales down

You are, in effect, a one-person endowment with a dated liability. If you plan to retire in 20 years and draw 4% of the portfolio a year after that, you owe a spending stream with a start date. Write it down the way Yale would: spend rate, inflation assumption, required nominal return, and the years until the first withdrawal. That sentence will set your allocation in Lesson 4, your risk budget in Lesson 9, and your benchmark in Lesson 3. Without it, every later decision is a matter of taste.
