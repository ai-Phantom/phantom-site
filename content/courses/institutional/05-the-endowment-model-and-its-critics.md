## What the model is

The "endowment model", or Yale model, is the approach David Swensen built at Yale from 1985 and described in *Pioneering Portfolio Management*. Its logic has four parts. A perpetual, tax-exempt investor with no redemptions has a comparative advantage in bearing illiquidity, so it should be paid for doing so. Public markets are efficient enough that skill is hard to find and expensive to buy, whereas private markets, venture and some hedge fund strategies are inefficient enough that the gap between top-quartile and median managers is enormous, so the return comes from manager selection. Diversification across many return sources reduces risk more than holding bonds does, and bonds are held only for crisis liquidity. And equity-like assets should dominate, because the liability (spending plus inflation, forever) needs equity-like returns.

The result is a portfolio that looks nothing like a 60/40 fund. Yale's fiscal 2021 targets put 64.5% in absolute return, venture capital and leveraged buyouts combined, and 2.25% in US public equity. Harvard's FY2025 report shows 41% private equity, 31% hedge funds, 14% public equities, 5% real estate, 4% bonds and TIPS, 3% other real assets and 3% cash.

The results have been strong at the institutions that invented the model. Yale reports 9.5% a year for the decade to June 2024 and 10.3% for two decades, ahead of the median endowment by 2.4 and 3.0 points respectively and ahead of a 70/30 stock-bond portfolio over both spans. Harvard reports 9.6% a year over its current management team's eight years to June 2025, against its 8% target.

## The 2008 lesson

Fiscal 2009 (July 2008 to June 2009) was the model's stress test. Harvard's endowment returned −27.3% and fell from $36.9 billion to $26.0 billion; Yale's returned −24.6% and fell from $22.9 billion to $16.3 billion. Those are big losses, but a perpetual investor can survive a 25% drawdown. The lesson was about liquidity, not return, and it had three parts.

First, the **denominator effect**. Public equities fell 30–40% and were marked daily; private partnerships were marked quarterly and with a lag, so on paper they fell far less. With no new purchases at all, the illiquid share of endowment portfolios rose above policy limits mechanically, because the denominator (total assets) shrank faster than the illiquid numerator.

Second, **unfunded commitments**. Private equity and venture funds call capital over several years. Endowments had committed billions that could be called at any time, and the general partners kept calling, because the same crisis produced cheap deals. The cash had to come from the liquid part of the portfolio, which was the part that had just fallen.

Third, **the spending did not fall**. Universities had budgeted a distribution based on smoothed values; the smoothing rule meant the dollar amount barely moved while the endowment fell a quarter. Harvard's spending had also grown to fund a large share of the operating budget, so cutting it meant cutting the university.

The combination forced actions the model was supposed to prevent: Harvard issued $2.5 billion of taxable bonds in December 2008 to raise liquidity, halted construction, and sold private stakes on the secondary market at discounts; several endowments did the same. Yale cut spending and reduced future commitments. The board-level conclusion, adopted almost everywhere afterwards, was that liquidity has to be managed as its own risk: unfunded commitments count against the illiquidity limit, a liquidity reserve is sized to cover several years of spending plus expected calls, and the stress test assumes public markets fall while private ones do not reprice.

## The critics

The critique has two strands, one about returns and one about who can do this.

On returns, Lerner, Schoar and Wang (2008) documented that endowment outperformance was concentrated in the largest and most selective institutions; endowments outside the top tier holding the same asset classes earned much less, which points to manager access and selection rather than the asset classes as the source of the premium. If the edge is access to the top decile of venture and buyout funds, the model cannot be copied by writing the same allocation on a page.

Richard Ennis and others have argued that since the financial crisis the average endowment has failed to beat a simple public-market blend of comparable risk after fees, that private-market marks understate true volatility and overstate diversification, and that the large fees paid (2-and-20 and worse, Lesson 7) have consumed most of the illiquidity premium. Harvard's FY2025 report itself notes that results were "dampened by having less public than private equity" in a year when public equity ran ahead, and CalPERS's 10-year return to June 2024 was 6.2% against Yale's 9.5%, which shows how wide the dispersion among heavy alternative allocators is.

Even the defenders concede a point: Swensen's second book, *Unconventional Success* (2005), written for individuals, tells them to use low-cost index funds in a handful of asset classes and to avoid the fee-heavy products that market themselves as endowment-style. The model works for Yale because Yale is Yale.

## Worked example

Run the fiscal 2009 stress test on Yale's own fiscal 2021 targets, so every input is a published number.

**Starting point.** Illiquid assets (venture 23.5% + buyouts 17.5% + real estate 9.5% + natural resources 4.5%) = 55.0% of the portfolio. Liquid and semi-liquid assets (absolute return 23.5% + foreign equity 11.75% + bonds and cash 7.5% + domestic equity 2.25%) = 45.0%. Yale's policy ceiling on illiquid assets is 50%; the target already exceeds it on paper, which is why Yale manages the limit including private-asset marks and commitments rather than at target weights. Use 50/50 as the starting portfolio for a clean test, on a $100 endowment.

**Shock.** Liquid assets fall 30% (roughly the fiscal 2009 public-equity outcome). Illiquid assets are marked down 5% (the lagged mark).

- Liquid: 50 × 0.70 = 35.0
- Illiquid: 50 × 0.95 = 47.5
- Total: 82.5; illiquid share = 47.5 / 82.5 = 57.6%

Nothing was bought, and the portfolio is now 7.6 points over its 50% ceiling.

**Spending.** The distribution was set on smoothed values, so it stays near 5% of the pre-shock value: 5.0 from the liquid pool.

- Liquid: 35.0 − 5.0 = 30.0
- Total: 77.5; illiquid share = 47.5 / 77.5 = 61.3%

**Capital calls.** Suppose unfunded commitments equal to 15% of the pre-shock endowment are called at the usual pace of one-third a year: 5.0 more from the liquid pool.

- Liquid: 30.0 − 5.0 = 25.0
- Illiquid: 47.5 + 5.0 = 52.5
- Total: 77.5; illiquid share = 52.5 / 77.5 = 67.7%

In one year, with no decision made, the portfolio went from 50% illiquid to 68%, and the liquid pool fell from 50 to 25, or half of one more year of spending plus calls. That is the arithmetic behind Harvard's December 2008 bond issue. The fix institutions adopted is visible in the numbers: count the 15% of commitments against the limit up front (so the starting position is 65% "illiquid or committed", which fails the constraint immediately), and hold a liquidity reserve equal to at least two years of spending plus expected calls, here about 20% of the endowment, in assets that do not fall 30%.

## What to take from it

The endowment model is a real edge for a small number of institutions with perpetual horizons, top-tier access and the discipline to manage liquidity as a constraint. For everyone else, the transferable parts are the diversification across return sources, the insistence on a written liquidity limit that counts commitments, and the stress test above. The non-transferable part is the belief that buying "alternatives" through retail vehicles delivers Yale's return. Lesson 12 makes that list explicit.
