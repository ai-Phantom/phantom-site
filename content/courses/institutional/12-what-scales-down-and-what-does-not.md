## The honest inventory

This course has spent eleven lessons on how professional money is run. The last question is which parts of it are worth copying at $500,000. The answer is not "all of it", and it is not "none of it". Some institutional habits cost nothing and work at any size; others depend on scale, access or a structure you do not have, and copying them through retail substitutes usually leaves you paying institutional fees for retail results. The architect of the endowment model said as much: Swensen's book for individuals, *Unconventional Success*, recommends a handful of low-cost index funds, rebalanced, and warns against the fee-laden products that market themselves as endowment-style.

Here is the inventory, five and five.

## Five habits that scale down

**1. Start from the liability and write the IPS.** Lessons 1 and 2. A one-page policy with a return objective derived from what you need to spend, a drawdown you have decided you can carry, constraints, policy weights with bands, a benchmark and a review date. It costs nothing, and it does the same job for you that it does for Yale: it converts panic into a memo. The institutional version has a board; yours has a 30-day cooling-off clause.

**2. Allocate by policy, then leave it alone.** Lesson 4. Ibbotson and Kaplan's result, that policy explains most of what happens to a portfolio over time, holds regardless of size. Choose weights from published capital-market assumptions against your objective, put them in the IPS, and treat tactical views as bounded, dated and scored.

**3. Rebalance by rule and use cash flows first.** Lesson 8. A calendar check with bands is as effective for a $500,000 account as for a $500 billion one, and cheaper: your contributions are a larger share of the portfolio than a pension's, so most of your rebalancing can be done with new money and no trades, which in a taxable account also means no realized gains.

**4. Measure the way institutions measure.** Lessons 3 and 10. Time-weighted return against a policy blend you wrote down in advance; money-weighted return once a year to score your own timing; a two-line Brinson attribution to see whether allocation or selection did the work; and fee per unit of active share on every fund you own. None of this requires software beyond a spreadsheet.

**5. Manage liquidity as a budget and stress it.** Lessons 5 and 8. A tier list of what you can sell today, in a month, and not at all; two years of any planned withdrawal covered by the first two tiers; and a once-a-year stress test in which everything liquid falls 30% and nothing illiquid can be sold. Unfunded commitments (a house purchase, a private investment's capital calls) count against the limit.

## Five that do not

**1. Private-market manager selection.** Lessons 5 and 7. The endowment model's return comes from access to top-quartile venture and buyout funds on negotiated terms. Individuals reach private markets through feeder funds, interval funds and listed vehicles that add a fee layer and offer the managers who need retail capital, which are not the ones Yale is in. The worked example below prices this.

**2. Leverage and risk parity.** Lesson 9. Institutions borrow at rates near cash, with collateral terms set by their scale. Retail margin is expensive, callable and, as 2022 showed, most dangerous exactly when the diversification it funds stops working.

**3. Hedge funds at 2-and-20.** Lesson 7. Ben-David, Birru and Rossi's finding that investors kept 36 cents of every dollar earned was measured on institutional investors with negotiating power. Retail access to the same strategies comes with worse terms, less transparency and no operational due diligence.

**4. Tactical asset allocation with a staff.** Lesson 4. Even with teams, data and consultants, Brinson's average timing contribution was negative. Without them, the base rate does not improve. Bound it, date it, score it, or skip it.

**5. Committee governance and consultants.** Lesson 11. The structure exists to stop any one person betting the fund, at the cost of herding and career risk. You do not need the structure; you need its output, which is rules written before the decision. Copy the rules, not the meetings.

## Worked example

Price the endowment model for an individual, using only numbers from earlier lessons.

**Step 1: the institutional policy return.** Lesson 4 mapped Yale's fiscal 2021 targets onto J.P. Morgan's 2025 assumptions and got 7.29% a year, versus 6.07% for a 70/30 blend of US large cap (6.7%) and aggregate bonds (4.6%). Edge on paper: 1.22 points.

**Step 2: the retail fee layer.** Yale's 65% in absolute return, venture and buyouts reaches the individual through feeder or interval funds. J.P. Morgan's alternative-asset assumptions are already net of institutional fees, so the retail layer is incremental. Assume 1.5 points a year (a wrapper management fee plus an incentive share; many listed products charge more). Drag: 0.65 × 1.5 = 0.98 points. Individual's expected return: 7.29 − 0.98 = 6.31%.

**Step 3: the access gap.** Lerner, Schoar and Wang found the endowment premium concentrated in the largest, best-connected institutions. Harris, Jenkinson and Kaplan found buyout funds beat public equity by about 3 points a year on average, with a wide spread between quartiles. If the retail-accessible managers deliver median rather than top-quartile results, a conservative haircut on the private sleeves of 1 point a year is another 0.41 × 1.0 = 0.41 points (venture plus buyouts = 41% of the policy). Expected return: 6.31 − 0.41 = 5.90%.

**Step 4: compare.** The 70/30 index blend costs about 0.05% a year (SPY's expense ratio is 0.0945% and AGG's 0.03%; a total-market fund like VTI is 0.03%): net about 6.02%. The retail endowment copy: 5.90%, with half the portfolio locked up for years, quarterly marks that hide volatility, and capital calls that count against the liquidity budget.

**Step 5: over 20 years on $500,000.**
- 70/30 index at 6.02%: 500,000 × 1.0602^20 = $1,610,000 (1.0602^20 = 3.220).
- Retail endowment copy at 5.90%: 500,000 × 1.0590^20 = $1,573,000 (1.0590^20 = 3.146).
- Yale itself at 7.29%: 500,000 × 1.0729^20 = $2,042,000 (1.0729^20 = 4.085).

The individual who copies the allocation ends up roughly where the index blend does, with far worse liquidity, and $470,000 short of what the model earns at Yale. The gap is fees and access, not the allocation, which is exactly Swensen's point.

**Step 6: what the individual can copy instead.** The capstone's reference allocation (Lesson 13) reaches an expected compound return of about 6.45% at 10.96% volatility using only index funds, by taking the diversification lesson (international equity, REITs, TIPS) without the private-market wrapper. That is within 0.9 points of the Yale policy at a tenth of the cost and full liquidity, which is the deal an individual can actually get.

## The one advantage you have

An institution's staff can be fired for being right too early. You cannot. The only structural edge an individual has over Yale is the absence of career risk: you can hold a policy through a 2017–2020 value drawdown or a 2022 bond year without explaining it to anyone. That edge is worth more than any product, and it is only real if the policy is written down before the drawdown begins. That is what the capstone asks you to do.
